\begin{abstract}
    % Discuss > Problem -> Electrification couples paratransit mobility, batteries, chargers and the grid, and drivers control the mobility. Planning studies either replay observed mobility or simulate driver decisions.
    % Discuss > Design -> Three minibus-taxi communities in Cape Town, selected by a frozen rule from 11 communities that are found in router GPS data.
    % Discuss > Methods -> Data replay (\MR) and a mixed-fleet agent-based model (\MA) with the full corridor population; every charging design is evaluated in both models.
    % Discuss > Results -> One sentence per RQ: outcome difference (E1), design difference (E2--E4), cost of the replay design and EV passenger loss (E5), consistency over communities (E6, synthesis).
    % Discuss > Implication -> When driver agency matters for pilot charging infrastructure.
    Electrifying minibus taxis requires charging infrastructure that accommodates both vehicle energy requirements and the operational decisions of drivers. An existing planning approach uses data-replay models to assess whether observed mobility is compatible with electrification. However, it remains unclear whether introducing explicit representations of driver agency changes the charging infrastructure that is selected. This study compares a data-replay model with a decision-adaptive agent-based model for three minibus-taxi communities in Cape Town, South Africa, selected from eight communities identified using vehicle GPS telemetry. Both models electrify the same observed vehicles, while the agent-based model also represents synthetic internal combustion engine vehicles competing for passengers on the same corridors. Every candidate charging design is evaluated using the model-specific measures of operational performance.

    Under a common reference design selected by the data-replay model, 57.0\%, 22.6\% and 49.0\% of vehicle-days end in stranding in Khayelitsha, Bellville and Wynberg, respectively. No validated agent-based run strands, because drivers can travel to charging stations. However, under the reference design, the electrified agents carry 18.5\%, 6.0\% and 23.8\% fewer passengers than in their internal-combustion counterfactuals. When each model selects its own design, the agent-based model increases installed charging power from 250 to 1,000 kW in Khayelitsha, retains 200 kW but reallocates the connectors in Bellville, and increases installed power from 100 to 150 kW in Wynberg. When evaluated in the agent-based model, the data-replay designs produce lower EV passenger throughput than the optimised agent-based designs in all three communities. These differences persist across the tested sensitivity cases.
    
    The results show that replay-based feasibility assessments do not necessarily identify the charging design that best supports passenger service when drivers can adapt. Agent-based modelling captures this additional operational response, but requires substantially more computation and explicit assumptions about driver behaviour and passenger demand. The appropriate modelling approach therefore depends on the planning question and the operational constraints of the proposed electrification pilot.
    % Editorial note > The phrase "these differences persist across the tested sensitivity cases" is deliberately narrower than claiming general robustness. The analysis covers three selected communities and six sensitivity cases, not the full range of possible operating conditions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Paratransit electrification as a coupled planning problem}

% Discuss > Establish that electrification couples previously weakly coupled systems: mobility <-> battery <-> charging <-> grid. and that paratransit adds another important coupling: electrification constraints <-> driver behaviour.
% - Slow charging, limited range and finite chargers create opportunity costs that did not exist in the same form for ICE vehicles.

Paratransit systems, such as minibus taxis, are a critical component of urban mobility in many cities, particularly in developing countries where large gaps remain in formal public transport provision~\cite{behrens2015_paratransit, bhuyan2024_paratransit_services_and_socio_econ_planning}. As global efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions intensify, the electrification of these systems has become a focal point for sustainable urban transport planning~\cite{collett2021_ev_good_ssa, nasac2024_decarbonising_transport_in_africa, bankole2026_electrifying_paratransit_ssa}. However, the transition from internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles to electric vehicles (EVs) introduces a complex set of interdependencies that were previously weakly coupled. Specifically, the mobility of vehicles is now closely linked to vehicle drive-train efficiency, battery capacity, and charging infrastructure, which in turn affects the electrical grid and drivers' ability to service passenger demand~\cite{booysen2025_sys_of_sys_em_planning}. This coupling creates new challenges for planning and operation, as the limitations of EVs, such as slower charging times and limited range, changes the operational status quo, introducing opportunity costs that were not present for internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles. When investigating the electrification of paratransit system, it is therefore essential that the planning models account for the interactions between these systems, as well as the adaptive behaviour of drivers who respond to these constraints in their operational decisions~\cite{}.
% TODO: Include Gaurav citation when available.

% Discuss > Driver agency -> Drivers control much of the underlying mobility through decisions about where, when and how long to operate.

In South Africa, minibus-taxi drivers have significant agency over their operational decisions, including where, when, and how long to operate~\cite{behrens2015_paratransit, ndibatya2020_kampalas_minibus_taxi_operations, vegter2020_sa_mbt_industry, schlueter2020_stochastic_mbt_behaviour_prediction}. This agency allows drivers to adapt their mobility in response to changing passenger demand, traffic conditions, and other operational constraints~\cite{schlueter2020_stochastic_mbt_behaviour_prediction, ndibatya2021_characterising_mbt_movement}. With the introduction of electrification, drivers must also learn to adapt their behaviour in response to the constraints imposed by electrification, such as limited range and charging availability~\cite{booysen2025_sys_of_sys_em_planning}. As a result, the success of electrification initiatives is not solely dependent on the technical feasibility of EVs but also on how drivers respond to these new operational constraints. Understanding and modelling driver behaviour is therefore crucial for accurately predicting the outcomes of electrification and for designing effective charging infrastructure that meets both operational and passenger demand requirements.


% > Discuss > Electrification pilots in a mixed fleet
% - Pilots are community-scale, not fleet-scale -> The first electric minibus taxis will be a small group of vehicles that share stops, routes and hubs. A charger goes where these vehicles stop, so the pilot unit is a vehicle community, not a city fleet.
% - Early EVs compete with ICE vehicles -> On the same corridors, most vehicles stay ICE. Time at a charger is time in which an ICE competitor can carry the passenger. Data replay cannot represent this loss; an agent-based model with the full corridor population can.
% - Conclude -> The comparison of the two paradigms is most relevant at the scale and in the market in which pilots will happen.
% - Tie in next section in last sentence

Furthermore, because of significant economical, infrastructural, and operational barriers, mixed fleets are likely to be the norm in the early stages of paratransit electrification~\cite{pretorius2024_scheduling_mixed_fleets}, where the first electric minibus taxis will be a small group of vehicles that share stops, routes, and hubs, rather than an entire electrified fleet. This means that, for the purposes of this study, the planning and evaluation of electrification initiatives are conducted at the community scale, where the interactions between electric and internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles are particularly relevant. In such a mixed-fleet environment, the time spent by EVs at charging stations represents an opportunity cost, as ICE competitors can continue to carry passengers during these periods. Furthermore, vehicle communities are not necessarily aligned with taxi associations or licensed routes, and the study does not assign any association to a vehicle or community. Therefore, the comparison of fixed-mobility data-replay models and adaptive-mobility agent-based models is most relevant at the scale and in the market in which electrification pilots are likely to occur. Thus, understanding the implications of modelling paradigms and driver agency with respect to the interactions between electric and ICE vehicles is crucial for designing effective charging infrastructure and ensuring the success of electrification initiatives in paratransit systems.


\subsection{Modelling paratransit: the tale of two paradigms}
% Discuss > Rehash the discussion had in the Gaurav paper + Booysen et al. sys-of-sys paper. Establish that the modelling of paratransit is a complex problem, and that the modelling of electrification is a complex problem, and that the two problems are coupled.

Modelling the electrification of paratransit systems is a complex problem that requires careful consideration of both technical and behavioural factors. In the domain of assessing the integration of EVs into paratransit systems, two primary modelling paradigms have emerged: fixed-mobility data-replay models and adaptive-mobility agent-based models. Each paradigm offers distinct advantages and limitations, and the choice between them can significantly impact the predicted outcomes of electrification initiatives. 

% Discuss > State the methodological tention -> Data replay is strongly grounded in observed behaviour but assumes that mobility largely persists after electrification. Agent-based modelling can represent adaptive responses, but introduces behavioural assumptions, calibration requirements, computation, and uncertainty.

Planning models are normative tools that shape transitions and encode assumptions about which outcomes planners can observe and which they cannot~\cite{}. The fixed-mobility data-replay paradigm is strongly grounded in observed behaviour, providing a direct assessment of whether the existing mobility patterns are compatible with electrification. However, it assumes that mobility will largely persist after electrification, potentially overlooking the adaptive responses of drivers to new constraints. In contrast, the adaptive-mobility agent-based paradigm can represent these adaptive responses, allowing for a more dynamic assessment of how drivers may alter their operations in response to electrification, as well as evaluating well defined counterfactual scenarios. However, this approach introduces additional behavioural assumptions, calibration requirements, computational complexity, and uncertainty into the modelling process. As a result, the choice between these two paradigms can significantly influence the predicted outcomes of electrification initiatives, particularly in terms of charging infrastructure requirements and passenger service levels. 

\subsection{Research gap}
% Discuss > Frame as -> Existing studies establish electrification feasibility under observed mobility or develop behaviourally richer simulation frameworks, but there is limited evidence on whether the two representations of driver agency lead to different infrastructure decisions at pilot scale, and whether replay-derived solutions remain robust when drivers respond and compete with ICE vehicles.
This research investigates the implications of driver agency for the planning of paratransit electrification pilots. Existing techniques can evaluate whether observed paratransit mobility is compatible with electrification~\cite{}, while behaviourally explicit models can represent adaptive responses to energy and charging constraints~\cite{}. What remains unclear is whether these different representations lead to materially different infrastructure planning decisions when applied to the same empirically observed vehicles, and whether infrastructure selected under fixed-mobility assumptions remains effective once drivers respond endogenously and compete for passengers with the internal-combustion vehicles that share their corridors. It is also unclear whether such differences are consistent across the distinct operating communities in which electrification pilots are likely to take place.

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{sec:research_questions}

This study poses and addresses the following research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For the same observed community vehicles, how do predicted electrification outcomes differ when mobility is represented as fixed empirical activity (\MR) versus endogenous driver behaviour (\MA) under a common reference charging design?
    \item Does endogenous driver behaviour change the charging infrastructure that is selected when each model is evaluated using the outcomes it can validly represent?
    \item What does a replay-derived charging design cost in passenger service when it is evaluated in the agent-based model, and what share of passengers do the electric vehicles lose to internal-combustion competitors while they charge?
    \item Do these answers point in the same direction in three different vehicle communities, and how sensitive are they to the corridor population and the vehicle assumptions?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}
% Discuss > Keep this paragraph short; list the contributions in the order of the RQs.
This study examines the implications of explicit behavioural representations of driver agency for paratransit electrification planning. It contributes to the literature in five ways. First, it performs a controlled comparison of fixed-mobility data replay and decision-adaptive agent-based modelling, in which the same observed vehicles are electrified in both models, in three minibus-taxi communities in Cape Town. Second, it introduces the vehicle community, found from router GPS data and screened for electrification by a rule that is frozen before any agent-based run, as a reproducible unit for the selection of electrification pilots. Third, it simulates the full corridor population in the agent-based model, with EVs in a mixed fleet, which makes the passengers that EVs lose to internal-combustion competitors measurable. Fourth, it evaluates every charging design in both models, so the Pareto fronts are exact and the comparison does not depend on the performance of a search algorithm. Fifth, the comparison framework adheres to model-native objective spaces, rather than forcing a single objective space onto models with different behavioural assumptions.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Electrification feasibility under observed mobility}
\label{sec:rw_replay}
% Discuss > P1: Formal fleets -> Trajectory replay is the established feasibility test. Range adequacy of private vehicles from observed driving; e-taxi charging siting from taxi GPS traces. Trips are fixed and charging demand is inferred from where vehicles already stop.

Assessing the feasibility of electrifying a fleet of vehicles requires an understanding of the energy requirements of the vehicles and the availability of charging infrastructure. The most common way to obtain both is to replay the observed movement of the fleet through an EV energy model. GPS logs and travel surveys of private vehicles show that most daily driving fits within the range of an affordable EV that charges once a day~\cite{pearre2011_ev_range_required}, and the dwell patterns of taxi fleets have been used to site and size charging stations~\cite{cai2014_beijing_taxi_charging_siting, tu2016_etaxi_charging_location, yang2017_etaxi_siting_sizing}. In each case the trips are fixed, and a vehicle can charge only where it already stops.

% Discuss > P2: SSA paratransit -> The same approach is the default for minibus taxis, because vehicle tracking is the only data that records the duty cycle on which electrification depends. Lineage: data case, energy, mobility simulation, solar and grid, interdependence, equity, viability, Cape Town transition, measured vehicle performance.

For minibus taxis in sub-Saharan Africa, data replay has become a significant planning method, partly because vehicle tracking is the only data that records the duty cycle on which electrification depends~\cite{rix2022_taxi_tracking_embt_planning}. Tracked vehicles have been used to estimate energy consumption~\cite{hull2023_high_fidelity_paratransit_energy, giliomee2023_simulating_embt_mobility, abraham2025_empirically_validated_embt_sim, sello2026_ekamva_performance}, to assess charging opportunities with grid and solar supply~\cite{abraham2021_solar_embt_charging, booysen2022_embt_load_shedding,sello2026_embt_in_cpt}, and to measure the share of vehicle-days that remain viable under different charging designs~\cite{pretorius2025_viability_existing_mbt_mobility, pretorius2024_grid_mobility_interdependence, booysen2026_cpt_embt_transition}, which can differ sharply between groups of drivers in the same town~\cite{pretorius2024_uneven_transitions}. In Cape Town, replay underpins estimates of energy demand, emissions and operator costs~\cite{sello2026_embt_in_cpt}. More recently, trip GPS trajectories have been used to empirically measure the energy consumption of the first commercial electric minibus taxi in South Africa~\cite{sello2026_ekamva_performance}.

% Discuss > P3: The assumption -> Every study above assumes that mobility persists after electrification. A vehicle-day that cannot be completed is written off rather than adapted, so mixed-fleet conclusions follow partly from the assumption. Scheduled-mobility models relax the assumption by optimising operation centrally, but they describe a reformed system rather than current operation. Neither paradigm represents drivers competing for passengers.

Underlying these studies is the assumption that mobility persists after electrification. A vehicle-day that cannot be completed on battery power is written off rather than adapted, so the conclusion that a mixed fleet is needed follows partly from the assumption itself~\cite{}. % TODO: Gaurav paper. 
Scheduled-mobility models relax this assumption by optimising vehicle schedules and charging centrally, including the joint deployment of electric and ICE vehicles~\cite{pretorius2024_scheduling_mixed_fleets, wust2025_mbt_scheduling}, but they describe a reformed system rather than current operation~\cite{booysen2025_sys_of_sys_em_planning}. Neither paradigm represents drivers who compete for passengers, so neither can measure the passengers that an EV loses to ICE competitors while it charges. Data replay therefore answers whether today's trips can be electrified, not how operation would change once they are.

\subsection{Behavioural simulation of taxi and paratransit fleets with charging}
\label{sec:rw_abm}
% Discuss > P1: Formal fleets -> Agent-based and discrete-event models of electric taxi, ride-hailing and autonomous fleets let vehicles decide when and where to charge. Observed e-taxi fleets show that drivers organise charging around their shifts and favour a few stations. Charging-behaviour assumptions alone change the estimated infrastructure need by a factor of 2.3 to 4.5, yet supply-side planning studies still use simplistic behaviour.
% TODO: It would be nice to include the thredbo19 paper in this paragraph
Agent-based and discrete-event simulations relax the persistence assumption by letting vehicles decide when and where to charge. Simulations of electric taxi and ridesourcing fleets show that EVs can match the service of ICE fleets given adequate charging infrastructure~\cite{bischoff2014_etaxi_abm, bauer2019_ridesourcing_electrification}, and simulations of autonomous fleets have been used to jointly size the fleet and its charging facilities~\cite{chen2016_saev_fleet_charging, yang2023_eamod_fleet_charging_design}. Observed charging supports this focus on behaviour: in Shenzhen's fully electric taxi fleet, drivers organise their charging around their shifts and concentrate on a few stations~\cite{lei2022_etaxi_charging_dynamics}. How this behaviour is represented also changes the infrastructure that is planned. In an agent-based simulation of Sweden, realistic charging archetypes required 2.3 to 4.5 times more public chargers per vehicle than refuelling-style charging~\cite{liao2023_charging_behaviour_infra_needs}, yet supply-side planning studies still rely mostly on simplistic behavioural assumptions~\cite{patil2023_charging_behaviour_infra_review, vanderkam2019_abm_charging_behaviour}.

% Discuss > P2: Paratransit -> Shorter history. MATSim minibus operators; paratransit response to formal transit; Kampala paratransit as a complex adaptive system; empirical evidence on driver labour supply and movement; one framework for electric minibus taxis with adaptive drivers.

Paratransit simulation has a shorter history. Co-evolutionary operator models in MATSim reproduce the main minibus corridors of a South African metropolitan area~\cite{neumann2015_towards_mbt_simulation_in_sa}, agent-based simulation shows that paratransit operators gain most from long headways in formal transit~\cite{joubert2020_behavioural_response_of_paratransit}, and Kampala's minibus taxis have been modelled as a complex adaptive system~\cite{ndibatya2021_paratransit_abm_thesis}. These models are consistent with evidence that drivers' working hours respond partly to daily revenue~\cite{schlueter2020_stochastic_mbt_behaviour_prediction} and that drivers search widely for passengers~\cite{ndibatya2021_characterising_mbt_movement}, but none of them represents electric vehicles. To our knowledge, only one framework simulates electric minibus taxis whose drivers choose their routes, waiting and charging in response to remembered demand and battery state~\cite{sello2025_stellenbosch_embt_abm}, and this study builds on it.

% Discuss > P3: Cost and blind spot -> Behavioural realism costs calibration against observed patterns, which replay avoids. Formal-fleet models usually simulate a fully electric fleet or a single operator, so passengers lost to ICE competitors while an EV charges are not measured. Mixed paratransit fleets have been treated as a scheduling problem, not as competition.

The price of behavioural realism is calibration. An agent-based model must reproduce several observed patterns at once~\cite{thiele2014_abm_parameter_estimation}, and there is no generally accepted procedure for validating it~\cite{collins2024_abm_validation_methods}, a burden that replay avoids by construction. The formal-fleet models also simulate either a fully electric fleet or a single operator, so they cannot measure the passengers that an EV loses to ICE competitors while it charges. In paratransit, mixed fleets have so far been treated as a scheduling problem rather than as competition~\cite{pretorius2024_scheduling_mixed_fleets, wust2025_mbt_scheduling}. Yet early pilots will place a few EVs among many ICE vehicles on the same corridors, where this competition determines what electrification costs the drivers who adopt it first.

\subsection{Paratransit networks and operating communities from GPS data}
\label{sec:rw_gps}
% Discuss > P1: GPS and phone traces map paratransit where no formal route data exists: open network data, route typologies, trip counts per road segment, movement patterns. Where paratransit has no formal route data, GPS and phone traces have been used to reconstruct it. Mobile-phone mapping produced open network data for Nairobi's matatus~\cite{williams2015_digital_matatu}, and collaborative mapping in Nairobi and Maputo has made minibus systems visible in planning~\cite{klopp2019_mapping_minibuses}. In South Africa, more than 500 Cape Town routes have been clustered into trunk, intermediate and feeder routes~\cite{dupreez2019_paratransit_route_typology}, and GPS traces from Tshwane have been extrapolated to minibus-taxi trip counts per street segment~\cite{debeer2025_quantifying_paratransit}, while traces from Kampala reveal how drivers search for passengers~\cite{ndibatya2021_characterising_mbt_movement}. This work builds on general methods for stop detection and trajectory mining~\cite{zheng2015_trajectory_data_mining}, and its units of analysis are routes, corridors and road segments. Vehicles have been grouped by home location~\cite{pretorius2024_uneven_transitions}, but not by the hubs and corridors that they share, which is the unit at which a charging pilot would be planned.

\subsection{Comparing models of different structure}
\label{sec:rw_comparison}
% Discuss > P1: Docking and alignment -> An established practice in agent-based modelling, usually aimed at showing that two models agree on common outputs. In eMBT work, two energy-simulation tools have been reconciled. Replay and ABM do not share an output, and multi-fidelity methods assume they do. Model choice is not neutral: it fixes which outcomes planners can see. This motivates model-native selection followed by cross-evaluation.

Comparing models of different structure is an established practice in agent-based modelling. Docking tests whether two models of the same system produce equivalent results, at the level of numerical, distributional or relational equivalence~\cite{axtell1996_aligning_simulation_models}, and independent replication can expose artefacts that a single implementation hides~\cite{collins2024_abm_validation_methods}. In minibus-taxi electrification, the alignment of two energy-simulation tools reconciled consumption estimates that differed by a factor of more than two~\cite{abraham2023_aligned_embt_simulation}. These comparisons ask whether models agree on a common output. Data replay and agent-based simulation do not share one: replay reports whether a vehicle-day strands, whereas the agent-based model, for this study, focuses on how many passengers an EV carries. The choice of model therefore shapes which outcomes planners can see~\cite{saltelli2020_models_serve_society, booysen2025_sys_of_sys_em_planning}. % TODO: Gaurav paper.
A fair comparison lets each model select a design by its own measures and then evaluates both designs in the model that can represent the outcomes of both.

\subsection{Minibus-taxi operation in Cape Town}

Minibus taxis carry the largest share of the public-transport trips in Cape Town~\cite{}. Their operation is shaped by vehicle owners, route associations and the provincial operating-licence system~\cite{}. Five operating characteristics are particularly relevant to electrification and to the models developed in this study.

First, passengers commonly board at taxi ranks, where vehicles queue on a first-in, first-out basis and depart when sufficiently full~\cite{}. This is known as fill-and-go loading. Second, passengers may board or alight at stops along a route, although boarding is generally concentrated at terminals. Third, vehicles tend to operate on a limited set of routes between established terminals~\cite{}. Fourth, drivers may wait or move slowly along a route to find passengers, a practice referred to here as hold-back~\cite{}. Finally, demand is directional and varies over the day~\cite{}. During the morning peak, many passengers travel from residential areas towards employment centres, leaving some taxis with relatively few passengers on the return journey~\cite{}. The pattern often reverses in the evening.

These features create specific charging constraints. Long dwell periods at ranks and terminals provide potential charging opportunities, but a taxi that leaves a loading queue to charge may lose its position and the passengers it would otherwise have carried. The models therefore need to represent both the availability of charging opportunities and the cost of using them.

Shown in Figure~\ref{fig:study_context}a, the study area is the union of the City of Cape Town metropolitan municipality and the surrounding Stellenbosch and Drakenstein municipalities. Telemetry data outside the area is flagged but not deleted. Around 19\% of the driven distance lies outside he study area, and a vehicle-day with 50~km or more outside the area is considered a long-distance day.

\subsection{Vehicle telemetry}
\label{sec:telemetry}

The observed fleet dataset is a subset of the Sebenza router telemetry~\cite{sebenza2025_telemetry}, which is a GPS dataset of minibus taxis in South Africa. The subset consisted of 378 minibus taxis operating in Cape Town over 11 weekdays from 1 to 15 December 2025, resulting in a total of 3,916 vehicle-days. The second half of December was excluded because of the school holidays and the Christmas period which produce a distinct mobility regime~\cite{sello2026_gau_mbt_distance}. Each GPS point includes a pseudonymised vehicle identifier, a timestamp, a position, and a quality flag. The median sampling interval is 79 seconds, with the 5th to 95th percentile ranging from 60 to 81 seconds. On average, a moving taxi covers a median distance of 435 meters between two points. While the start, end, and overnight positions of each day are observed, stops shorter than 2 to 3 minutes, which include most boarding and alighting events along a route, are not captured in the dataset.

Table~\ref{tab:cpt_fleet_summary} summarises the duty cycle of the observed Cape Town fleet. The median valid vehicle-day covers 190~km (interquartile range 130 to 247~km), whereas the battery window of the study EV gives about 130~km; only 24.9\% of the valid days fall within this range. The median driving window, from the departure after the first stop of the day to the arrival at the last, is 17.4~h. The median taxi therefore parks for about 6.6~h, in which a 3.7~kW socket adds at most about 22~kWh, less than a third of the median daily need of 72~kWh. Further analysis shows that long dwells concentrate at ranks, official roue termini, and stations. Additionally, each vehicle varies strongly from day to day: the intraclass correlation is 0.43 (95\% confidence interval 0.36 to 0.50) for the daily distance and 0.62 (0.56 to 0.67) for the daily moving time, thus 57\% of the variance of the daily distance lies between the days of one vehicle.

\subsection{Observation period, data quality and analysis sample}
\label{sec:study_days}
Established by \citet{pretorius2024_grid_mobility_interdependence}, an operational vehicle-day runs from 04:00 to 03:59 the following morning. This boundary groups overnight charging with the operating cycle that precedes it, since most taxis are parked during this period. A GPS step is treated as evidence of data loss, and the vehicle-day is marked invalid, when consecutive observations are more than 600 seconds apart and more than 1,000~m apart. A long interval without corresponding movement is instead treated as a parked period.

Of the 3,916 observed vehicle-days, 2,446 (62.5\%) satisfy the validity criteria. The valid share varies from 29\% to 87\% across dates, while the number of active vehicles remains comparatively stable at 351--360 per day. This pattern suggests that variation in the valid share reflects data completeness more than changes in fleet activity. The agent-based model therefore applies a day-level correction factor when scaling observed activity to daily operation.

The study days fall into two classes (Table~\ref{tab:cpt_fleet_summary}). The eight term weekdays (1 to 5 and 8 to 10 December) end with the last school day, and the three school-holiday weekdays (11, 12 and 15 December) follow it~\cite{dbe2023_school_calendar}. The holiday weekdays have three times the share of long-distance days (7.5\% against 2.5\%) and a longer mean distance (214 against 193~km), whereas without the long-distance days the two classes are almost the same (mean 184 against 185~km). They therefore represent a different demand regime and are excluded from every fit. Of the term weekdays, Tuesday 2 and Wednesday 10 December, with valid shares of 79\% and 86\%, are the hold-out days, and the other six are the calibration days. Section~\ref{sec:methodology_design} gives the steps that use each class. No candidate community is excluded on data quality. Their valid shares are 59\% to 75\%, their gap times 1\% to 2\%, and their shares of driving outside the network 14\% to 47\%.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Comparative design and shared assumptions}
\label{sec:methodology_design}

The study evaluates the same observed vehicles using two models, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}. In the data-replay model (\(\MR\)), each vehicle follows its observed movements. In the agent-based model (\(\MA\)), drivers generate realistic movements through operational decisions and can respond to energy and charging constraints. The electrified agents in \(\MA\) compete for passengers with synthetic ICE vehicles operating on the same corridors.

The representation of vehicle movement is the primary difference under investigation. Both models use the same vehicle and charging assumptions, candidate sites and design space, and both electrify the same observed vehicles within each community. Where their operating periods differ, the evaluation windows are made as comparable as the model structures allow. One asymmetry remains: \(\MR\) allows vehicles to charge during dwell periods that include waiting in a rank loading queue, whereas \(\MA\) requires drivers to leave the queue to charge. This assumption favours \(\MR\) and should be considered when interpreting the results.

\subsubsection{Vehicle and charging values}
\label{subsubsec:vehicle_assumptions}
Both models use one assumption set based on the Higer H5C, a 15-seat electric minibus~\cite{higer_h5c_ev_2022}. The vehicle has a usable battery capacity (\( C \)) of 70.47~kWh, an empirically measured energy consumption rate of 0.38~kWh/km in loaded operation~\cite{sello2026_ekamva_performance}, a vehicle charging limit of 60~kW and a charging efficiency of 90\%. The battery operates between a reserve of \( 0.2\,C \) and a charge limit of \( 0.9\,C \). This window holds 49.3~kWh, or about 130~km. Every vehicle charges overnight at 3.7~kW from a socket outlet, and site chargers serve vehicles in a first-come, first-served queue.

\subsubsection{Unit of analysis and study days}
\label{subsubsec:unit_of_analysis}

The unit of analysis is the vehicle community: a group of vehicles with overlapping operating areas, routes and hubs. These shared operating patterns create overlapping charging requirements and make communities a useful scale for evaluating electrification pilots. A community is a data-derived operating group, not a taxi association, fleet operator or licensed route.

Network construction, route inference and community detection use all 11 observation weekdays. Agent-based model calibration uses the six designated calibration days, while the two hold-out days remain unused until the calibrated model has been accepted. Since \(\MR\) has no fitted behavioural parameters, it replays all eight term weekdays.

\subsubsection{Planning principle}
The study selects one charging design per model and community. The selection principle is to achieve service performance close to the best observed within the model's feasible design set while minimising the required infrastructure. Installed power, calculated as the number of connectors multiplied by their rated power, serves as a proxy for infrastructure cost.

The service measure depends on the model. In \(\MR\), the selection rule uses the share of vehicle-days that remain viable under repeated operation, as described by \citet{pretorius2024_grid_mobility_interdependence}. In \(\MA\), it uses the passenger throughput of the electrified vehicles, alongside constraints on expired passenger requests. The precise selection rules are given in Section~\ref{sec:objectives}. Since the models represent different aspects of operation, the selected designs are subsequently evaluated in a common agent-based framework.

\subsubsection{Pipeline}
Figure~\ref{fig:system_diagram} summarises the study's methodology. GPS telemetry is used to infer the operational network, construct a route library and identify vehicle communities. A predefined screening rule selects three communities for further analysis (Section~\ref{sec:communities}). For each community, the same observed vehicles are electrified in both models.

The data-replay model evaluates every candidate charging design and selects a reference design, \(x_R\). The agent-based model searches the same design space using its own passenger-service and infrastructure measures, producing a selected design \(x_A\). Both \(x_R\) and \(x_A\) are then evaluated in the agent-based model. This cross-evaluation measures the operational consequences of selecting infrastructure under fixed-mobility assumptions. The analysis also examines whether these consequences persist across the three communities and under alternative vehicle energy-consumption, charging-policy and fleet-scale assumptions.

\subsection{Operational network, routes and vehicle communities}
\label{sec:methodology_network}

\subsubsection{Stop events and the operational network}
\label{sec:network}

Stop events are extracted from the GPS trajectories with an anchored-dwell algorithm that tolerates isolated positioning errors. An event has at least three inlier points within 30~m of its anchor and lasts at least 180~s. At the sampling interval of about 80~s, stops shorter than 2 to 3~min, which include most boarding and alighting along a route, are not observable. The home of a vehicle is the location within 300~m of which the first and last points of at least 75\% of its valid vehicle-days lie.

One operational network is inferred and applied to all communities. Its nodes come from evidence of stopping where grid cells with stop events from at least five vehicle-days and three vehicles, merged by complete linkage within 200~m, together with the formal ranks, termini and stations that the fleet uses. Its directed edges come from evidence of driving which constitute hops between nodes on at least two vehicle-days, their reverse edges, and links between nodes within 300~m. Edge lengths are shortest drivable paths on OpenStreetMap. After one manual review of 211 flagged items, the network has 726 nodes, of which 18 are ranks. It captures 76\% of the stop events and represents 66.5\% of the distance driven between stops with respect to the full fleet. 

\subsubsection{Route library and driver route sets}
\label{sec:routes}
% TODO: Add a reference for the Jaccard distance. 
Typically, minibus taxis load at a terminal and depart when full~\cite{behrens2015_paratransit}. A trip therefore runs between two detected anchor dwells, consisting of stop events of 10~min or more at non-home nodes. The trips of each terminal pair are grouped by the Jaccard similarity of their node sets, using a threshold of \( \theta = 0.5 \)~\cite{}. Each group with trips on at least three vehicle-days and two dates becomes a route, represented by its medoid trip. The fleet library holds 586 observed and 503 derived reverse routes. To limit the agent's decision space, the eligible routes of a driver is the smallest set of routes that covers 80\% of its trip distance on qualified routes, together with their reverse routes. Routes shorter than 5~km in the network are excluded. A vehicle with at least one eligible route becomes an agent of both models. In \MA, a route is one discrete unit of operation, where a driver cannot charge at a rank part-way along it.

\subsubsection{Vehicle communities and pilot selection}
\label{sec:communities}

Vehicle communities are identified from four views of observed operation: dwell time at network nodes, dwell time at formal ranks, use of network edges, and origin--destination pairs of anchor dwells. Features are weighted using term frequency--inverse document frequency (TF--IDF)~\cite{}. The similarity between two vehicles is calculated as the mean cosine similarity across the views available for both vehicles.

Each vehicle is connected to its 15 nearest neighbours in a similarity graph. The Leiden algorithm partitions this graph across a range of resolution parameters, using 50 random seeds at each resolution. The seed-level partitions are combined into a consensus partition~\cite{}. A resolution of 0.75 is selected, producing eight communities while providing the best stability among the resolutions that retain at least 90\% of corridor use within a single community.

Candidate charging sites are identified from each community's most frequently used non-home nodes. Nodes are ranked by the number of community vehicles with dwell periods of at least 20 minutes, and the first three are retained as candidate sites in both models. Each community is then screened using the energy core of \(\MR\) under three charging scenarios: overnight charging only; overnight charging plus unrestricted 60 kW charging at the three candidate hubs; and depot only charging. The corresponding viable shares of vehicle-days are denoted by \(V_1\), \(V_2\) and \(V_3\). For each candidate hub, the estimated connector requirement is based on the 95th percentile of the observed daily peak number of simultaneous charging sessions in the second scenario. This screening rule was established to avoid biasing the outcomes of the data-replay agent-based comparison.

A community qualifies for pilot selection when it contains at least 15 vehicles, has a formal main hub, benefits materially from hub charging (\(V_2-V_1\geq15\) percentage points), and has eligible routes that represent at least 30\% of the median driver's trip distance. The last condition ensures that the agent-based model represents a meaningful share of community operation. The three qualifying communities with the highest \(V_2\) values are selected, subject to having distinct main hubs. Consequently, the results apply most directly to communities that meet these electrification-readiness criteria, rather than to all minibus-taxi communities. 

\subsection{Fixed-mobility data-replay}
\label{sec:replay}

The data-replay model (\(\MR\)) follows the trajectory-replay approach used in earlier minibus-taxi electrification studies~\cite{pretorius2024_grid_mobility_interdependence,booysen2026_cpt_embt_transition}. Each observed vehicle-day is replayed using the energy-consumption and battery assumptions of the study EV~\cite{sello2026_ekamva_performance, higer_h5c_ev_2022}. Trajectories, departure times and dwell periods remain fixed. A vehicle can charge when it is parked near an available charger but cannot divert from its observed movement to reach one.

A charging opportunity is defined as a stop lasting at least 10 minutes within 100~m of a candidate charging site. Vehicles from each date, called a fleet day, are replayed in chronological order, sharing the available connectors. A vehicle whose battery is below \(0.9C\) joins the first-in, first-out queue and charges at the configured depot charging power (\( P_o \)) and charger efficiency (\( \eta \)) until either its battery reaches \(0.9C\) or the observed stop ends. Since the replay uses observed dwell periods, a vehicle can charge during time spent waiting in a rank loading queue. This assumes that charging connectors are available at the loading bays. The agent-based model does not permit this as a driver must leave the loading queue to travel to a charging station. This asymmetry is retained in the comparison and favours the replay model. Additionally, every vehicle can also charge overnight, subject to the available parked time and the upper battery limit. The energy added overnight is

\begin{equation}
    E^{\text{night}} = \min\left(0.9\,C - E^{\text{end}},\ \eta P_o\, \Delta t_{\text{park}}\right), \qquad \Delta t_{\text{park}} = t^{\text{start}} + 24\,\text{h} - t^{\text{end}},
    \label{eq:overnight}
\end{equation}

\noindent where \( E^{\text{end}} \) is the battery energy at the end of driving, and \( t^{\text{start}} \) and \( t^{\text{end}} \) are the start and end of the driving window.

A vehicle-day is considered viable only if the vehicle can repeat the duty cycle without exhausting the battery. Each date is therefore simulated repeatedly, starting at \(0.9C\), until the daily starting battery levels converge to a fixed point or a sustainable cycle within a tolerance of \(0.001C\), all active vehicles have been resolved, or 100 iterations have been completed~\cite{booysen2026_cpt_embt_transition}. A vehicle that reaches zero battery energy strands and leaves the active set. Each vehicle-day is classified as viable, below reserve, stranded or unconverged. The stranded share is also reported for \(\MA\), where stranding is treated as a failure condition, providing a common reliability measure for the comparison.

\subsection{Decision-adaptive agent-based model}
\label{sec:abm}

The agent-based model (\(\MA\)) is implemented using the the discrete-event simulation framework established by \citet{sello2025_stellenbosch_embt_abm}. Each observed vehicle is represented by a driver agent who decides when to work, which route to operate, how long to wait for passengers, when to idle and, after electrification, when to charge. Unlike the replay model, \(\MA\) generates vehicle movement from these decisions.

Each simulated day represents a typical term weekday on the inferred operational network. The model specification follows the Overview, Design concepts and Details (ODD) protocol~\cite{grimm2010_odd_protocol,grimm2020_odd_second_update}, with the decision-making questions of its ODD+D extension~\cite{muller2013_odd_d}, and the full description is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:abm}. The following subsections focus on the mechanisms relevant to the comparison and on changes made relative to the earlier model.

\subsubsection{Operation}

When a driver is not servicing a route, the agent chooses between its eligible routes and idling using a multinomial logit model~\cite{mcfadden1974_conditional_logit,train2009_discrete_choice}. Route utility depends on the requests waiting at the current node, the number of competing drivers, and the driver's memory of route-level demand. This memory records the number of passengers served and their passenger-distance contribution during each 30-minute interval of the day. Idling becomes more attractive during intervals that the driver remembers as having low demand.

At each stop, the driver decides whether to continue waiting according to
\begin{equation}
    \pi = \left[ 1 + \exp\left(-\left(b + w_d\, d - w_o\, o\right)\right) \right]^{-1},
\end{equation}

\noindent where \(d\) is the remembered demand for the route, scaled by vehicle capacity, and \(o\) is the current onboard occupancy as a share of capacity. The parameters \(b\), \(w_d\) and \(w_o\) are calibrated. The initial stop of a route represents fill-and-go loading at a rank and has separate parameters, allowing drivers to wait according to their remembered demand at the terminal.

Relative to the earlier model by \citet{sello2025_stellenbosch_embt_abm}, the stop decision now uses route-specific demand memory, rank loading is represented explicitly, and includes a last-run rule that prevents a driver from starting a route that would extend beyond its shift. A passenger boards only if the vehicle is at the request's origin, has an available seat and serves the passenger's destination. Unserviced requests expire after two hours. Travel times are drawn from windowed log-normal distributions fitted to observed network hops, while each agent's shift is based on its observed first and last movements.

\subsubsection{Charging}

During the electrification experiments, the observed agents become EVs, while their synthetic ICE copies remain internal combustion vehicles. Each EV driver follows a demand-aware charging policy. The vehicle always charges when its state of charge falls below \(s_{\min}=0.2\), never charges above \(s_{\max}=0.9\), and charges between these limits when
% TODO: Make sure the notation is consistent with other papers.
\begin{equation}
    \frac{E(t)}{C} < s_{\min} + \left(s_{\max} - s_{\min}\right)\left(1 - \hat{h}_t\right),
\end{equation}

where \(E(t)\) is the battery energy and \(\hat{h}_t\in[0,1]\) is the normalised demand remembered by the driver for the current time interval. The policy makes charging more attractive when the driver expects charging to incur a smaller passenger-service opportunity cost.

Drivers can initiate charging only when they are not on a route. They travel to the nearest charging station and join its first-in, first-out queue. A driver waiting to load at a rank must leave the queue to charge and subsequently return to passenger service. Reserve rules reduce avoidable stranding: a driver may start a route only when the battery can support the route, the subsequent drive to the nearest station and the reserve of \(0.2C\). A route is not eligible if a full battery cannot support its completion. While a vehicle is at home, it can charge at 3.7~kW. If an EV strands, it loses its onboard passengers and restarts from home with a full battery on the following day.

\subsubsection{Run horizon}
A cold simulation begins with empty driver memories and includes a warm-up period of 49--91 days. The warm-up ends when the seven-day means of passenger-service and route-choice measures remain close to their long-run means for three random seeds. The accepted warm-up is stored and reused for each experiment within a community. Each experiment begins from this saved warm start and runs for 14 settling days, allowing EV drivers to learn their charging behaviour. These are followed by 14 evaluation days, which provide the outcomes used for design selection and comparison.

\subsection{Mixed-fleet population and passenger demand}
\label{sec:demand}

The GPS dataset covers only a subset of the taxis operating from each hub --- those fitted with Sebenza Wi-Fi routers. Thus, to represent a larger corridor population, \(\MA\) adds synthetic ICE copies of the observed agents. The resulting population is, on average, \(k\) times the number of agents. Each copy follows its associated agent's eligible routes, idle locations and shift pattern, but is assigned a nearby home location and a shift adjusted by a random draw with a standard deviation of 30 minutes. This construction assumes that the observed agents provide a useful representation of the wider population operating from the same hub. The electrified agents account for 2.6\%--6.6\% of the simulated driver population (Table~\ref{tab:models}), reflecting the small share expected in an initial pilot. The remaining synthetic vehicles retain ICE drivetrains and compete with the EVs for passenger requests.

Since GPS trajectories do not reveal passenger demand directly, the model generates a latent demand process using observed operating patterns and published passenger counts. The observed departures, routes and stops determine the structure, direction and timing of demand, while daily boardings at the main hub in the CITP~\cite{coct2023_citp} determine its overall scale. Each departure has a load factor of \(\lambda=1\), except for counter-peak travel during the morning and afternoon peaks. For those departures, the load factor is set to the counter-peak-to-peak ratio of vehicle trips in CITP Table 3-18, which ranges from 0.09 to 0.39. The demand scale factor is

\begin{equation}
    k = \frac{B}{Q\,L},
\end{equation}

\noindent where \(B\) is the published daily number of boardings at the main hub, \(Q\) is vehicle capacity and \(L\) is the estimated number of full agent loads from the hub per calibration day.

A share \(\alpha=0.8\) of passengers board at the route origin and alight downstream in proportion to the observed stop-event distribution. The remaining passengers travel between intermediate route nodes. Demand is generated only for origin--destination pairs that are served by at least one eligible route, follows the hourly operating profile of the agents and is sampled as a non-homogeneous Poisson process. This construction makes the simulated terminal boardings at the main hub equal the published boarding total by design.

The scale factor \(k\) is checked against an upper bound inferred from the provincial operating-licence register~\cite{wcpp2022_transport_public_works}. The Bellville estimate exceeds this bound, indicating uncertainty in the relationship between the observed agent population and the full hub population. This limitation is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion_limitations}; it should not be interpreted as evidence that the synthetic population is an exact reconstruction of the entire taxi service.

\subsection{Calibration and validation of the agent-based model}
\label{sec:calibration}

\subsubsection{Calibration}

The ICE baseline of each community is calibrated to reproduce observed operating patterns that determine an EV's daily energy requirement and opportunities for charging. Calibration targets include the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of daily distance, daily moving time and each agent's day-to-day coefficient of variation in daily distance. The hourly share of agents moving is also assessed, but is used as a realism check rather than a target that must be matched hour by hour. Additionally, the model must serve at least 95\% of passenger requests, and median seat occupancy should approach 0.6. These are plausibility constraints and goals rather than observed calibration targets. The fit of each target is expressed as its absolute error relative to the target's estimated sampling variability, calculated from 500 bootstrap resamples of complete calibration dates.

Following \citet{thiele2014_abm_parameter_estimation}, calibration proceeds in two stages. Hand tuning first identifies a plausible region of parameter space and reveals structural gaps that cannot be resolved by parameter adjustment alone. In Khayelitsha, these gaps required a travel-time scaling adjustment and the introduction of the last-run rule. A predefined search strategy then optimises nine behavioural parameters using a constrained, two-objective tree-structured Parzen estimator (TPE)~\cite{watanabe2026_tpe}. Selected configurations are validated through cold runs using three new random seeds. Finally, the accepted configuration is assessed against the held-out observation days. 

\subsubsection{Fidelity of the simulated movement}
\label{sec:representation}

The movement generated by \(\MA\) differs from the observed movement as agents operate on a simplified network, choose from a limited set of inferred routes and stop only at network nodes. Differences between the selected charging designs could therefore arise from the representation of movement, rather than from driver adaptation alone. To assess this effect, the data-replay rules are applied to the simulated movements from five validated ICE runs of \(\MA\). Each simulated home-to-home duty is treated as one vehicle-day. Distance is calculated from network travel, charging opportunities are identified from simulated dwell periods at candidate sites, and overnight energy is calculated using the same rule as in Equation~\eqref{eq:overnight}. The first eight evaluation days of each seed are used to match the number of days replayed in \(\MR\), and outcomes are averaged across the five seeds.

The resulting charging design, \(x_S\), is the design selected by the \(\MR\) rule when applied to simulated rather than observed movements. Comparing \(x_R\) with \(x_S\) isolates the effect of the movement representation under the replay selection rule. Comparing \(x_S\) with \(x_A\) captures driver adaptation together with the differences between the models' service measures and selection rules. The difference in EV passenger change between \(x_R\) and \(x_A\) is decomposed as

\begin{equation}
    \underbrace{\bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}}(x_R) - \bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}}(x_A)}_{\text{total}}
    = \underbrace{\bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}}(x_R) - \bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}}(x_S)}_{\text{representation}}
    + \underbrace{\bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}}(x_S) - \bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}}(x_A)}_{\text{behaviour}},
\end{equation}

\noindent where \( \bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}} \) is the mean EV passenger change of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ev_change}. The fidelity of the movement is reported with the quantities that the replay rules use: the daily distance, the overnight time, the charging opportunities at the sites and the ceiling of the not-stranded share. No pass threshold is set.


\subsection{Charging design experiment}
\label{sec:experiment}

\subsubsection{Candidate sites and design space}
\label{sec:design_space}

Each charging design is represented by

\begin{equation}
    x = (N_1, N_2, N_3, P_o), \qquad N_j \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 2\hat{N}_j\}, \qquad P \in \{22, 50\}\ \text{kW},
\end{equation}

\noindent where \(N_j\) is the number of connectors installed at candidate hub \(j\), and \(P_o\) is the rated power of each connector. Each hub can have between zero and \(2\hat{N}_j\) connectors, where \(\hat{N}_j\) is the connector estimate derived during screening. Connector power is either 22 or 50 kW. A value of \(N_j=0\) means that no charging site is installed at hub \(j\). Site selection is therefore part of the optimisation. The resulting design spaces contain 2,645 candidates in Khayelitsha, 1,169 in Bellville and 349 in Wynberg.

\subsubsection{Performance measures and selection rules}
\label{sec:objectives}
\label{sec:selection}

Each model evaluates designs using the measures it can represent, as summarised in Table~\ref{tab:objectives}. The data-replay model focuses on the share of vehicle-days that strand and the peak charging load across the candidate sites. The agent-based model reports these measures and also represents passenger demand, waiting times, expired requests and the passenger throughput of the EV agents.

In the replay model, the stranded share is the fraction of conclusive vehicle-days that end in stranding. The peak site load is the maximum simultaneous charging load summed across all sites. These measures define the replay model's design-selection objective space. In the agent-based experiments, every EV run is paired with an ICE counterfactual using the same configuration, random seed and warm start. In the counterfactual, the observed agents operate as ICE vehicles rather than EVs. Pairing these runs uses common random numbers to reduce simulation noise in the comparison.

The EV passenger change and the EV passenger loss are
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\mathrm{EV}} = \frac{M_{\mathrm{EV}}^{\text{EV run}}}{M_{\mathrm{EV}}^{\text{ICE run}}} - 1,
    \qquad
    \ell = 1 - \frac{R^{\text{EV run}}}{R^{\text{ICE run}}}, \qquad R = \frac{M_{\mathrm{EV}} / M}{Y_{\mathrm{EV}} / Y},
\end{equation}

\noindent where \( M \) is the number of passengers carried by all drivers on the evaluation days, \( Y \) is the time that all drivers spend away from home, and the subscript EV denotes the agents. \( \ell \) corrects \( \Delta_{\mathrm{EV}} \) for a change in the agents' time on the road. It is therefore a measure of passenger throughput relative to operating time, not a measure of passengers lost by the transport system as a whole.

The replay model is deterministic, allowing every candidate design to be evaluated. Its Pareto front is therefore exact within the specified design space. Because replayed vehicles cannot divert to a charging station, even unlimited connectors cannot make every vehicle-day viable. A design with 99 connectors at each candidate site is used to establish the attainable ceiling for the share of vehicle-days that avoid stranding. The replay candidates must reach at least 95\% of this ceiling. Among those candidates, \(x_R\) is selected by minimising peak site load and then installed power. The selected design is used as the common reference for the first research question.

The agent-based model is more computationally expensive, so it cannot feasibly evaluate every candidate design. Instead, a multi-objective TPE search evaluates 40 trials using one seed per trial, starting from \(x_R\). The search minimises expired passenger requests, mean passenger waiting time and peak site load, while maximising the passengers carried by the EV agents. The resulting candidates and \(x_R\) are then evaluated on five new seeds with their paired ICE counterfactuals. The final agent-based design, \(x_A\), is chosen using a one-standard-error rule. To avoid selecting a design solely because it carries marginally more passengers when that apparent advantage may be due to simulation randomness, a candidate must achieve an EV passenger change no more than one standard error below the best validated value, while its mean expired-request share must be within one percentage point of the minimum. Among qualifying candidates, the selected design has the lowest peak site load and then the lowest installed power. No design is selected manually. Any validated EV run that ends in stranding invalidates the selected result.


\subsubsection{Cross-evaluation, sensitivity and synthesis}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
\label{sec:synthesis}

The consequences of selecting infrastructure under fixed-mobility assumptions are measured by evaluating \(x_R\) and \(x_A\) in \(\MA\) using the same five validation seeds. For each outcome, the paired difference is calculated between the two designs on each seed and then averaged. The standard error of the mean paired difference quantifies the uncertainty across the five seeds. Since both designs use the same seed and warm start, the paired comparison reduces variation arising from different random simulation conditions. Differences within two standard errors of zero are treated as indistinguishable at the resolution of this experiment. 

Changes in expired requests are also examined. If EV passenger throughput falls while the overall expired-request share remains unchanged, the result is consistent with passengers being served by competing ICE taxis rather than being lost from the system. However, this inference should be interpreted alongside the passenger-service measures actually reported by the model.

Robustness is assessed by evaluating \(x_R\) and \(x_A\), without reselecting either design, under six alternative conditions: energy consumption 15\% below and above the baseline; charging-policy minimums of 0.1 and 0.35; and fleet scale factors \(k\) equal to 0.5 and 1.5 times their baseline values. Each case is evaluated using three seeds, with a corresponding ICE counterfactual where the changed condition affects ICE operation.

For each community, the analysis records three results: the difference in installed power between \(x_A\) and \(x_R\), the EV passenger-service cost of using \(x_R\) rather than \(x_A\), and the EV passenger change achieved by \(x_A\) relative to the ICE counterfactual. A result is considered robust when its direction is unchanged across all tested cases, and consistent across communities when the same direction occurs in all three. These experiments assess the robustness of the selected designs' outcomes. They do not test whether the designs themselves would remain optimal under the alternative conditions.

\subsection{Electrification outcomes under a common design (RQ1)}
\label{sec:results_rq1}

Under the reference design \(x_R\), the two models predict substantially different electrification outcomes (Table~\ref{tab:reference_design}). In the data-replay model, 57.0\%, 22.6\% and 49.0\% of vehicle-days strand in Khayelitsha, Bellville, and Wynberg, respectively. These failures largely reflect the mismatch between daily energy requirements and the charging opportunities available along the fixed trajectories. Median daily energy needs are 79, 55 and 70 kWh, whereas overnight charging at 3.7 kW supplies approximately 23, 19 and 19 kWh. Consequently, 94\%--97\% of vehicle-days require site charging to sustain repeated operation. Yet 29\%, 8\% and 33\% of days, respectively, contain no qualifying stop near a candidate charging site. Even with unlimited connectors, the replay model therefore predicts that 55\%, 19\% and 47\% of vehicle-days would still strand in the three communities (Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}).

Under the same reference design, site energy use is 2.8, 1.8 and 4.0 times the replay values in Khayelitsha, Bellville and Wynberg, respectively. The model also records 2.4, 1.5 and 3.8 times as many site charging sessions. The electrified agents nevertheless carry 18.5\%, 6.0\% and 23.8\% fewer passengers than in their corresponding ICE counterfactuals.

For this common design, the models predict the same peak site load because all installed connectors are used at the peak. The replay model reports lower site energy partly because stranded vehicles stop charging and leave the active fleet. \subsection{Selected charging infrastructure (RQ2)}
\label{sec:results_rq2}

The models select different charging designs in all three communities, and the replay-selected design \(x_R\) is not a qualifying candidate under the agent-based selection rule in any community (Table~\ref{tab:selected_designs} and Figure~\ref{fig:design_space}). The scale of the difference varies by community. In Khayelitsha, the agent-based model selects 1,000 kW of installed power, compared with 250 kW in the replay model. The selected design also has a higher peak site load: 800 kW rather than 250 kW. The agent-based search shows that additional connectors initially produce substantial passenger-service gains. The EV passenger loss falls by 46 percentage points between 22 and 250 kW of installed power, and by a further 14 points between 250 and 670 kW. These gains arise because drivers can travel to the charging sites. Under the replay model, the same additional capacity has little effect on stranding: the stranded share falls only from 57.0\% under \(x_R\) to 54.9\% under \(x_A\), since observed vehicles cannot divert to use the new capacity. Above 670 kW, the additional passenger-service gain is only 1.4 percentage points. Nevertheless, the selection rule chooses the 800 kW peak-load design because it has no direct cost penalty for installed capacity beyond the stated load-based criteria.

In Bellville, both models select 200 kW of installed power, but they allocate the connectors differently. The replay model assigns one connector to the first hub and three to the second, whereas the agent-based model assigns all four to the second hub. In Wynberg, the agent-based model selects 150 kW instead of 100 kW, with a different allocation between the first and third hubs. Above 150 kW, further connector additions do not reduce the EV passenger loss. The design differences are therefore not solely a question of total capacity. In Bellville and Wynberg, the location of the connectors matters even when the total installed power is similar.

\subsubsection{Representation and behaviour effects}

The simplified movement representation does not explain most of the design difference in Khayelitsha (Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}). When the replay selection rule is applied to simulated ICE movements, the resulting design \(x_S\) differs from \(x_R\) by only one connector in each community. In Khayelitsha, the remaining 800 kW difference between \(x_S\) and \(x_A\) is 16 times the 50 kW difference between \(x_R\) and \(x_S\). The result is therefore driven mainly by the operational responses and selection criteria represented in \(\MA\), rather than by the movement representation alone.

In Bellville and Wynberg, the difference in installed power between \(x_R\) and \(x_A\) is one connector or less. The principal change is the allocation of connectors between hubs. These results suggest that replay-based planning is most likely to understate infrastructure requirements where observed vehicles rarely stop at candidate sites but can gain passenger service by travelling to charge. However, the decomposition also combines behavioural effects with differences in the models' service measures and selection rules; it should not be interpreted as a pure causal estimate of behaviour alone.

\subsection{Cost of the replay design and the EV passenger loss (RQ3)}
\label{sec:results_rq3}

When evaluated in \(\MA\), the replay-selected design \(x_R\) results in an EV passenger change that is 15.4, 3.5 and 12.9 percentage points lower than the agent-based design \(x_A\) in Khayelitsha, Bellville and Wynberg, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:cross_evaluation}). Each difference exceeds two standard errors in magnitude. In Khayelitsha, the difference is primarily associated with the amount of installed charging capacity. In Bellville and Wynberg, connector location explains much of the difference.

The effect of location is apparent even when installed power is held constant. In Wynberg, the 100 kW allocation selected by the agent-based model performs better than the replay allocation, with a difference of 9.7 percentage points in EV passenger change. In Bellville, reallocating the same 200 kW of capacity produces a 3.5-percentage-point improvement. The representation component of the decomposition is comparatively small or acts in the opposite direction to the total effect (Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}). In Khayelitsha, \(x_S\) has one fewer connector than \(x_R\) and performs worse in passenger terms. The total difference between \(x_R\) and \(x_A\) therefore understates the improvement associated with the behaviour-and-selection component.

The EV passenger loss is not accompanied by a comparable increase in expired passenger requests. Under the two designs, the expired-request share is effectively unchanged in Khayelitsha and Bellville, and differs by only 0.1 percentage points in Wynberg. Mean passenger waiting time also changes by less than 10 seconds in each community. These results are consistent with ICE taxis absorbing much of the passenger demand that the EVs do not serve while charging.

Even under the agent-based design \(x_A\), EV passenger throughput remains below the ICE counterfactual. The time-adjusted EV passenger loss \(\ell\) is 3.2\% in Khayelitsha, 2.1\% in Bellville and 11.8\% in Wynberg. These remaining losses represent the operational cost of electrification under the selected charging infrastructure and the modelled mixed-fleet conditions. No validated EV run strands under either design. Figure~\ref{fig:passenger_loss} shows how passenger losses vary over the day and how they relate to charging activity.

\subsection{Robustness, consistency and computational cost (RQ4)}
\label{sec:results_rq4}

The difference ranges from 8.8 to 20.2 percentage points in Khayelitsha, 0.9 to 3.0 points in Bellville, and 4.6 to 18.4 points in Wynberg. Some Bellville differences are small relative to the variability across three simulation seeds, so the direction is more consistent than the magnitude.

Higher energy consumption generally widens the gap between the designs, while lower consumption narrows it. The additional overnight charging time in simulated movement gives EVs extra potential energy relative to observed movement. However, the advantage of the agent-based design persists even under the sensitivity case with 15\% lower energy consumption. Across all tested cases, only two EV-days strand, both in Bellville when the charging-policy minimum is reduced to \(s_{\min}=0.1\).

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{When does driver agency matter?}
% Discuss > Answer the title question from RQ1 to RQ4. Under which conditions (hub charging binding, competition density k) the two models select different designs.

The results show that driver agency matters when the observed movement does not provide sufficient charging opportunities to sustain repeated operation, hinders the use of additional installed capacity, or does not reflect the operational effects of charging on passenger service. For Khayelitsha, driver adaption changes both the amount of power and passenger service. The selected installed power rises from 250 kW to 1,000 kW, and the EV passenger loss falls from 19.2\% to 3.2\%. In Bellville, the selected installed power remains at 200 kW, but the connector allocation changes and the EV passenger loss falls from 5.3\% to 2.1\%. In Wynberg, both the installed power and connector allocation change, and the EV passenger loss falls from 25.2\% to 11.8\%. Subsequently, the results do not establish that the agent-based model is universally preferable but rather that it is more appropriate when designs can be reliably ascertained from calibrated operating patterns, inferred networks, synthetic fleet and latent passenger-demand assumptions. 

Within the context of electrifying Cape Town's paratransit, a particularly important distinction is that the agent-based model can represent the operational effects of charging on passenger service, while the replay model cannot. The agent-based model can capture how drivers adapt to charging opportunities and how this adaptation affects passenger throughput, which is crucial for evaluating the effectiveness of different charging designs. In contrast, the replay model assumes that drivers will follow their observed patterns without adaptation, potentially leading to suboptimal design choices.

\subsection{Implications for electrification pilots}
This study's findings show three key implications for electrification pilots. First, the results show that electrification pilots should consider the potential for driver adaptation when designing charging infrastructure for paratransit. Charging infrastructure should not be sized using range and observed trajectories alone when vehicles can change their movement patterns to access charging opportunities. Instead, pilots should account for the operational effects of charging on passenger service and the potential for drivers to adapt their routes and passenger seeking behaviour. Second, the results show that site allocation matters. For example, Bellville shows that two designs with the same installed power but different connector allocations can lead to different passenger service outcomes. Third, the consequences of charging extend beyond the electrified vehicles themselves. The results show that the EV passenger loss is not accompanied by a comparable increase in expired passenger requests, suggesting that ICE taxis absorb much of the passenger demand that the EVs do not serve while charging. This highlights the potential for electrification to undermine the ability of drivers to remain competitive in a mixed fleet, and the need to consider the broader transportation ecosystem when designing charging infrastructure to mitigate these effects.

These findings support evaluating candidate designs using both energy-feasibility measures and measures of passenger service. They do not, by themselves, establish which sites are technically or commercially feasible in practice. The study's candidate hubs, connector design space and charging assumptions remain a model-based representation of a possible pilot.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion_limitations}

Important limitations identified in this study include the selection of communities, the use of observed and synthetic fleets, the representation of networks and routes, calibration and behavioural assumptions, comparison between models, and temporal generalisability. The screening rule selects communities partly on their replay-based electrification outcomes, which may limit the applicability of findings to other communities. The electrified agents come from the observed telemetry subset, while the rest of the simulated fleet is constructed synthetically, which may affect the representativeness of the results. The inferred network does not capture all observed driving, and the eligible route sets cover only part of each agent's observed trip distance, limiting the scope of movements that can be reproduced. Calibration reproduces central duty-cycle statistics reasonably well but underestimates day-to-day variability and does not fully reproduce the hourly activity profile. The charging policy and latent passenger demand also affect outcomes. The two models have different representations of mobility, passenger service, and charging opportunities, which means that results should be interpreted as a comparison of modelling approaches rather than a controlled estimate of behaviour alone. Finally, three selected communities and a short December observation window cannot establish statistical generalisability across South African minibus-taxi operations or across different seasons. Reinforcing the argument made by \citet{collett2021_ssa_paratransit_decarbonisation_data}, the effectives of planning models aiding in the transition to electrified paratransit require a more systematic and wholistic approach to data collection to support the development of more generalisable models and findings.


\subsection{Future work}
% NOTE TO THINUS > I hope this does not undermine the inclusion of the Sebenza dataset, negating its large sample size by using Cape Town. I am trying to make it clear that the key limitation is more temporal. 
Future research should address the limitations identified in this study. Testing more communities and observation periods would help establish statistical generalisability across South African minibus-taxi operations and different seasons. Improving the representation of route and network coverage would allow for a more accurate assessment of charging infrastructure needs. At the time of writing, no electric minibus-taxi pilot has been implemented in South Africa, so the study relies on model-based representations of possible pilots. However, obtaining direct observations of charging and driver decisions would provide valuable data for calibrating models and validating assumptions. Incorporating constraints from the electricity distribution network would ensure that proposed charging designs are feasible within the existing infrastructure. Additionally, future work could compare the computational and planning value of replay and agent-based simulation across a wider range of candidate pilots, providing further insights into the most effective modelling approaches for electrification planning.

\section{Conclusion}
In conclusion, this study demonstrates that representing driver adaptation changed charging design selection in all three communities, but the form of the change depended on local operating patterns. In Khayelitsha, the agent-based model selected substantially more installed power; in Bellville, it selected a different connector allocation at the same total installed power; and in Wynberg, it selected both a different allocation and more installed power. Across all three communities, the replay-selected designs performed worse in terms of EV passenger throughput when evaluated in the agent-based model. These results suggest that replay-based analysis remains useful for evaluating electrification feasibility under observed mobility, but that it may not identify the charging design that best supports passenger service when drivers can adapt. Agent-based simulation can represent these additional operational effects, provided its demand assumptions, behavioural calibration and movement representation are assessed explicitly.
