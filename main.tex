    Electrifying minibus taxis requires charging infrastructure that accommodates both vehicle energy requirements and the operational decisions of drivers. An existing planning approach uses data-replay models to assess whether observed mobility is compatible with electrification. However, it remains unclear whether a fleet simulation model in which drivers' decision-making and agency are explicit suggests a different charging infrastructure configuration. This study compares a data-replay model with a decision-adaptive agent-based model for three minibus-taxi communities in Cape Town, South Africa, selected from eight communities identified using vehicle GPS telemetry. Both models electrify the same observed vehicles. The agent-based model also simulates the internal combustion engine (ICE) taxis on the same routes, which can carry the passengers that an electric vehicle (EV) misses while it charges. Each model selects a charging design with its own measures of operational performance, and each selected design is then evaluated in the other model.

    % CHANGE: "No agent-based run of the comparison strands" -> "In the base case, ..." (R2 self-review #19: some sensitivity runs strand).
    Under a common reference design selected by the data-replay model, 57.0\%, 22.6\% and 49.0\% of vehicle-days end in stranding in Khayelitsha, Bellville and Wynberg, respectively. In the base case, no agent-based run strands, because drivers can travel to charging stations. However, under the reference design, the EV agents carry 18.1\%, 6.9\% and 26.4\% fewer passengers than in their ICE counterfactuals. When each model selects its own design, the agent-based model increases installed charging power from 250 to 650 kW in Khayelitsha, and keeps the same installed power but moves the connectors to other hubs in Bellville and Wynberg. When evaluated in the agent-based model, the data-replay designs carry 11.9, 3.5 and 10.3 percentage points fewer EV passengers than the agent-based designs. Where the share can be measured, ICE competitors take 85\% to 98\% of the passengers that the EVs lose. The differences persist in five of the six sensitivity cases in Bellville and in all cases in the other communities. In Khayelitsha, the additional capacity pays for itself at a fare of about R14 a trip; in Bellville and Wynberg, the agent-based design costs less and carries more passengers.

    The results show that replay-based feasibility assessments do not necessarily identify the charging design that best supports passenger service when drivers can adapt. Agent-based modelling captures this additional operational response, but requires about three orders of magnitude more computation and explicit assumptions about driver behaviour and assumptions or information about passenger demand. The appropriate modelling approach therefore depends on the planning question and the operational constraints of the proposed electrification pilot.

\section{Introduction}
\subsection{Paratransit electrification as a coupled planning problem}

% Discuss > Establish that electrification couples previously weakly coupled systems: mobility <-> battery <-> charging <-> grid. and that paratransit adds another important coupling: electrification constraints <-> driver behaviour.
% - Slow charging, limited range and finite chargers create opportunity costs that did not exist in the same form for ICE vehicles.
% CHANGE: "140~km electric minibuses" -> the 130~km battery window of this study (R2 self-review #15: the paper used 140 and 130 km for the same quantity).

Paratransit systems, such as minibus taxis, are a critical component of urban mobility in many cities, particularly in developing countries where large gaps remain in formal public transport provision~\cite{behrens2015_paratransit, bhuyan2024_paratransit_services_and_socio_econ_planning}. As global efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions intensify, the electrification of these systems has become a focal point for sustainable urban transport planning~\cite{collett2021_ev_good_ssa, nasac2024_decarbonising_transport_in_africa, bankole2026_electrifying_paratransit_ssa}. However, the transition from internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles to electric vehicles (EVs) introduces a complex set of interdependencies that were previously weakly coupled. Specifically, the mobility of vehicles is now closely linked to vehicle drive-train efficiency, battery capacity, and charging infrastructure, which in turn affects the electrical grid and drivers' ability to service passenger demand~\cite{booysen2025_sys_of_sys_em_planning}. This coupling creates new challenges for planning and operation, as the limitations of EVs, such as slower charging times (440~km/min for diesel compared to 1.2--3.9~km/min for electric minibuses~\cite{booysen2025_sys_of_sys_em_planning, sello2026_ekamva_performance}) and limited range (840~km for diesel~\cite{booysen2025_sys_of_sys_em_planning} compared to about 130~km for the battery window of the electric minibus of this study, Section~\ref{subsubsec:vehicle_assumptions}), naturally change the operational status quo, introducing opportunity costs that were not present for internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles. When investigating the electrification of paratransit systems, it is therefore essential that the planning models account for the interactions between these systems, as well as the adaptive behaviour of drivers who respond to these constraints in their operational decisions~\cite{sello2026_planning_paradigms, waraich2013_phev_smart_grids, sheppard2017_beam}.

% Discuss > Driver agency -> Drivers control much of the underlying mobility through decisions about where, when and how long to operate.

In South Africa, minibus-taxi drivers have significant agency over their operational decisions, including where, when, and how long to operate~\cite{behrens2015_paratransit, ndibatya2020_kampalas_minibus_taxi_operations, vegter2020_sa_mbt_industry, schlueter2020_stochastic_mbt_behaviour_prediction}. This agency allows drivers to adapt their mobility in response to changing passenger demand, traffic conditions, and other operational constraints~\cite{schlueter2020_stochastic_mbt_behaviour_prediction, ndibatya2021_characterising_mbt_movement}. With the introduction of electrification, drivers must also learn to adapt their behaviour in response to the constraints imposed by electrification, such as limited range and charging availability~\cite{booysen2025_sys_of_sys_em_planning}. As a result, the success of electrification initiatives is not solely dependent on the technical feasibility of EVs but also on how drivers respond to these new operational constraints. Understanding and modelling driver behaviour is therefore crucial for accurately predicting the outcomes of electrification and for designing effective charging infrastructure that meets both operational and passenger demand requirements.

% > Discuss > Electrification pilots in a mixed fleet
% - Pilots are community-scale, not fleet-scale -> The first electric minibus taxis will be a small group of vehicles that share stops, routes and hubs. A charger goes where these vehicles stop, so the pilot unit is a vehicle community, not a city fleet.
% - Early EVs compete with ICE vehicles -> On the same corridors, most vehicles stay ICE. Time at a charger is time in which an ICE competitor can carry the passenger. Data replay cannot represent this loss; an agent-based model with the full corridor population can.
% - Conclude -> The comparison of the two paradigms is most relevant at the scale and in the market in which pilots will happen.
% - Tie in next section in last sentence

Furthermore, because of significant economic, infrastructural, and operational barriers, mixed fleets are likely to be the norm in the early stages of paratransit electrification~\cite{pretorius2024_scheduling_mixed_fleets}, where the first electric minibus taxis will be a small group of vehicles that share stops, routes, and hubs, rather than an entire electrified fleet. This means that, for the purposes of this study, the planning and evaluation of electrification initiatives are conducted at the community scale, where the interactions between electric and internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles are particularly relevant. In such a mixed-fleet environment, the time spent by EVs at charging stations represents an opportunity cost, as ICE competitors can continue to carry passengers during these periods. Furthermore, vehicle communities are not necessarily aligned with taxi associations or licensed routes, and the study does not assign any association to a vehicle or community. Therefore, the comparison of fixed-mobility data-replay models and adaptive-mobility agent-based models is most relevant at the scale and in the market in which electrification pilots are likely to occur. Thus, understanding the implications of modelling paradigms and driver agency with respect to the interactions between electric and ICE vehicles is crucial for designing effective charging infrastructure and ensuring the success of electrification initiatives in paratransit systems.


\subsection{Modelling paratransit: the tale of two paradigms}
% Discuss > Rehash the discussion had in the Gaurav paper + Booysen et al. sys-of-sys paper. Establish that the modelling of paratransit is a complex problem, and that the modelling of electrification is a complex problem, and that the two problems are coupled.

Modelling the electrification of paratransit systems is a complex problem that requires careful consideration of both technical and behavioural factors. In the domain of assessing the integration of EVs into paratransit systems, two primary modelling paradigms have emerged: fixed-mobility data-replay models and adaptive-mobility agent-based models. Each paradigm offers distinct advantages and limitations, and the choice between them can significantly impact the predicted outcomes of electrification initiatives. 

% Discuss > State the methodological tention -> Data replay is strongly grounded in observed behaviour but assumes that mobility largely persists after electrification. Agent-based modelling can represent adaptive responses, but introduces behavioural assumptions, calibration requirements, computation, and uncertainty.
Planning models are normative tools that shape transitions and encode assumptions about which outcomes planners can observe and which they cannot~\cite{sello2026_planning_paradigms, saltelli2020_models_serve_society}. The fixed-mobility data-replay paradigm is strongly grounded in observed behaviour, providing a direct assessment of whether the existing mobility patterns are compatible with electrification. However, it assumes that mobility will largely persist after electrification, potentially overlooking the adaptive responses of drivers to new constraints. In contrast, the adaptive-mobility agent-based paradigm can represent these adaptive responses, allowing for a more dynamic assessment of how drivers may alter their operations in response to electrification, as well as evaluating well defined counterfactual scenarios. However, this approach introduces additional behavioural assumptions, calibration requirements, computational complexity, and uncertainty into the modelling process. As a result, the choice between these two paradigms can significantly influence the predicted outcomes of electrification initiatives, particularly in terms of charging infrastructure requirements and passenger service levels. 

\subsection{Research gap}
% Discuss > Frame as -> Existing studies establish electrification feasibility under observed mobility or develop behaviourally richer simulation frameworks, but there is limited evidence on whether the two representations of driver agency lead to different infrastructure decisions at pilot scale, and whether replay-derived solutions remain robust when drivers respond and compete with ICE vehicles.
% CHANGE: "infrastructure selected under fixed-mobility assumptions" -> "a charging design suggested by a fixed-mobility model" (supervisor R2 #41: ambiguous about the act of selection); "corridors" -> "routes" (R2 #4).
This research investigates the implications of driver agency for the planning of paratransit electrification pilots. Existing techniques can evaluate whether observed paratransit mobility is compatible with electrification, while behaviourally explicit models can represent adaptive responses to energy and charging constraints. What remains unclear is whether these different representations lead to materially different infrastructure planning decisions when applied to the same empirically observed vehicles, and whether a charging design suggested by a fixed-mobility model remains effective once drivers respond endogenously and compete for passengers with the internal-combustion vehicles that share their routes. It is also unclear how large such differences are, and what form they take, across the distinct operating communities in which electrification pilots are likely to take place.

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{sec:research_questions}

% CHANGE: RQ2 asks whether the agent-based model suggests a different configuration (supervisor R2 #41: "infrastructure that is selected" is ambiguous); RQ4 "corridor population" -> "number of drivers on the same routes" (R2 #4).
This study poses and addresses the following research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For the same observed community vehicles, how do predicted electrification outcomes differ when mobility is represented as fixed empirical activity (\MR) versus endogenous driver behaviour (\MA) under a common reference charging design?
    \item Does a model in which drivers' decisions are explicit suggest a different charging infrastructure configuration from a data-replay model, when each model judges designs by the outcomes it can validly represent?
    \item What does a replay-derived charging design cost in passenger service when it is evaluated in the agent-based model, and what share of passengers do the electric vehicles lose to internal-combustion competitors while they charge?
    \item How large is the difference between the models in three different vehicle communities, what form does it take (more capacity, connectors at other hubs, or both), and how sensitive are the size and the form to the number of drivers on the same routes and the vehicle assumptions?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}
% Discuss > Keep this paragraph short; list the contributions in the order of the RQs.
% CHANGE: "the full corridor population" -> "every driver on the routes of the electrified vehicles" (supervisor R2 #4: "corridor" has a specific civil-engineering meaning).
This study examines the implications of explicit behavioural representations of driver agency for paratransit electrification planning. It contributes to the literature in five ways. First, it introduces a comparison framework in which each model selects a charging design in its own model-native objective space, and each selected design is then evaluated in the other model, rather than forcing a single objective space onto models with different behavioural assumptions. Second, it applies this framework as a controlled comparison of fixed-mobility data replay and decision-adaptive agent-based modelling, in which the same observed vehicles are electrified in both models, in three minibus-taxi communities in Cape Town. Third, it introduces the vehicle community, found from router GPS data and screened for electrification by a rule that is frozen before any agent-based run, as a reproducible unit for the selection of electrification pilots. Fourth, it simulates every driver on the routes of the electrified vehicles in the agent-based model, with EVs in a mixed fleet, which makes the passengers that EVs lose to internal-combustion competitors measurable. Fifth, it enumerates every candidate design in the replay model, so the replay Pareto front is exact, and it validates the agent-based candidates on paired seeds and evaluates the selected designs on seeds that the selection did not use.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Electrification feasibility under observed mobility}
\label{sec:rw_replay}
% Discuss > P1: Formal fleets -> Trajectory replay is the established feasibility test. Range adequacy of private vehicles from observed driving; e-taxi charging siting from taxi GPS traces. Trips are fixed and charging demand is inferred from where vehicles already stop.

Assessing the feasibility of electrifying a fleet of vehicles requires an understanding of the energy requirements of the vehicles and the availability of charging infrastructure. The most common way to obtain both is to replay the observed movement of the fleet through an EV energy model. GPS logs and travel surveys of private vehicles show that most daily driving fits within the range of an affordable EV that charges once a day~\cite{pearre2011_ev_range_required}, and the dwell patterns of taxi fleets have been used to site and size charging stations~\cite{cai2014_beijing_taxi_charging_siting, tu2016_etaxi_charging_location, yang2017_etaxi_siting_sizing}. In each case the trips are fixed, and a vehicle can charge only where it already stops.

% Discuss > P2: SSA paratransit -> The same approach is the default for minibus taxis, because vehicle tracking is the only data that records the duty cycle on which electrification depends. Lineage: data case, energy, mobility simulation, solar and grid, interdependence, equity, viability, Cape Town transition, measured vehicle performance.

For minibus taxis in sub-Saharan Africa, data replay has become a significant planning method, partly because vehicle tracking is the only data that records the duty cycle on which electrification depends~\cite{rix2022_taxi_tracking_embt_planning}. Tracked vehicles have been used to estimate energy consumption~\cite{hull2023_high_fidelity_paratransit_energy, giliomee2023_simulating_embt_mobility, abraham2025_empirically_validated_embt_sim, sello2026_ekamva_performance}, to assess charging opportunities with grid and solar supply~\cite{abraham2021_solar_embt_charging, booysen2022_embt_load_shedding,sello2026_embt_in_cpt}, and to measure the share of vehicle-days that remain viable under different charging designs~\cite{pretorius2025_viability_existing_mbt_mobility, pretorius2024_grid_mobility_interdependence, booysen2026_cpt_embt_transition}, which can differ sharply between groups of drivers in the same town~\cite{pretorius2024_uneven_transitions}. In Cape Town, replay underpins estimates of energy demand, emissions and operator costs~\cite{sello2026_embt_in_cpt}. More recently, trip GPS trajectories have been used to empirically measure the energy consumption of the first commercial electric minibus taxi in South Africa~\cite{sello2026_ekamva_performance}.

% Discuss > P3: The assumption -> Every study above assumes that mobility persists after electrification. A vehicle-day that cannot be completed is written off rather than adapted, so mixed-fleet conclusions follow partly from the assumption. Scheduled-mobility models relax the assumption by optimising operation centrally, but they describe a reformed system rather than current operation. Neither paradigm represents drivers competing for passengers.

Underlying these studies is the assumption that mobility persists after electrification. A vehicle-day that cannot be completed on battery power is written off rather than adapted, so the conclusion that a mixed fleet is needed follows partly from the assumption itself~\cite{sello2026_planning_paradigms}. Scheduled-mobility models relax this assumption by optimising vehicle schedules and charging centrally, including the joint deployment of electric and ICE vehicles~\cite{pretorius2024_scheduling_mixed_fleets, wust2025_mbt_scheduling}, but they describe a reformed system rather than current operation~\cite{booysen2025_sys_of_sys_em_planning}. Neither paradigm represents drivers who compete for passengers, so neither can measure the passengers that an EV loses to ICE competitors while it charges. Data replay therefore answers whether today's trips can be electrified, not how operation would change once they are.

\subsection{Behavioural simulation of taxi and paratransit fleets with charging}
\label{sec:rw_abm}
% Discuss > P1: Formal fleets -> Agent-based and discrete-event models of electric taxi, ride-hailing and autonomous fleets let vehicles decide when and where to charge. Observed e-taxi fleets show that drivers organise charging around their shifts and favour a few stations. Charging-behaviour assumptions alone change the estimated infrastructure need by a factor of 2.3 to 4.5, yet supply-side planning studies still use simplistic behaviour.
% TODO: It would be nice to include the thredbo19 paper in this paragraph
Agent-based and discrete-event simulations relax the persistence assumption by letting vehicles decide when and where to charge. Agent-based transport simulations represent electric vehicles whose drivers plan charging around their activities, such as the electric vehicle extension of MATSim~\cite{waraich2013_phev_smart_grids, horni2016_matsim} and BEAM, which needed a detailed model of the charging decision to reproduce observed charging~\cite{sheppard2017_beam}; agent-based simulation has also been used to site public chargers~\cite{sheppard2016_charging_siting_abm}. Simulations of electric taxi and ridesourcing fleets show that EVs can match the service of ICE fleets given adequate charging infrastructure~\cite{bischoff2014_etaxi_abm, bauer2019_ridesourcing_electrification}, and simulations of autonomous fleets have been used to jointly size the fleet and its charging facilities~\cite{chen2016_saev_fleet_charging, yang2023_eamod_fleet_charging_design}. Observed charging supports this focus on behaviour: in Shenzhen's fully electric taxi fleet, drivers organise their charging around their shifts and concentrate on a few stations~\cite{lei2022_etaxi_charging_dynamics}. How this behaviour is represented also changes the infrastructure that is planned. In an agent-based simulation of Sweden, realistic charging archetypes required 2.3 to 4.5 times more public chargers per vehicle than refuelling-style charging~\cite{liao2023_charging_behaviour_infra_needs}, yet supply-side planning studies still rely mostly on simplistic behavioural assumptions~\cite{patil2023_charging_behaviour_infra_review, vanderkam2019_abm_charging_behaviour}.

% Discuss > P2: Paratransit -> Shorter history. MATSim minibus operators; paratransit response to formal transit; Kampala paratransit as a complex adaptive system; empirical evidence on driver labour supply and movement; one framework for electric minibus taxis with adaptive drivers.

Paratransit simulation has a shorter history. Co-evolutionary operator models in MATSim reproduce the main minibus corridors of a South African metropolitan area~\cite{neumann2015_towards_mbt_simulation_in_sa}, agent-based simulation shows that paratransit operators gain most from long headways in formal transit~\cite{joubert2020_behavioural_response_of_paratransit}, and Kampala's minibus taxis have been modelled as a complex adaptive system~\cite{ndibatya2021_paratransit_abm_thesis}. These models are consistent with evidence that drivers' working hours respond partly to daily revenue~\cite{schlueter2020_stochastic_mbt_behaviour_prediction} and that drivers search widely for passengers~\cite{ndibatya2021_characterising_mbt_movement}, but none of them represents electric vehicles. To our knowledge, only one framework simulates electric minibus taxis whose drivers choose their routes, waiting and charging in response to remembered demand and battery state~\cite{sello2025_stellenbosch_embt_abm}, and this study builds on it.

% Discuss > P3: Cost and blind spot -> Behavioural realism costs calibration against observed patterns, which replay avoids. Formal-fleet models usually simulate a fully electric fleet or a single operator, so passengers lost to ICE competitors while an EV charges are not measured. Mixed paratransit fleets have been treated as a scheduling problem, not as competition.
% CHANGE: "on the same corridors" -> "on the same routes" (supervisor R2 #4: "corridor" has a specific civil-engineering meaning).

The price of behavioural realism is calibration. An agent-based model must reproduce several observed patterns at once~\cite{thiele2014_abm_parameter_estimation}, and there is no generally accepted procedure for validating it~\cite{collins2024_abm_validation_methods}, a burden that replay avoids by construction. The formal-fleet models also simulate either a fully electric fleet or a single operator, so they cannot measure the passengers that an EV loses to ICE competitors while it charges. In paratransit, mixed fleets have so far been treated as a scheduling problem rather than as competition~\cite{pretorius2024_scheduling_mixed_fleets, wust2025_mbt_scheduling}. Yet early pilots will place a few EVs among many ICE vehicles on the same routes, where this competition determines what electrification costs the drivers who adopt it first.

\subsection{Paratransit networks and operating communities from GPS data}
\label{sec:rw_gps}
% Discuss > P1: GPS and phone traces map paratransit where no formal route data exists: open network data, route typologies, trip counts per road segment, movement patterns. Their units are routes, corridors and road segments. Vehicles have been grouped by home location, but not by shared hubs and corridors, which is the unit that would share chargers. Method citations (Leiden, consensus clustering, TF--IDF, Jaccard) stay in Section 4.
% CHANGE: "the hubs and corridors that they share" -> "the hubs and routes" (supervisor R2 #4: "corridor" has a specific civil-engineering meaning); "corridors" kept where it is the unit of the cited studies.

Where paratransit has no formal route data, GPS and phone traces have been used to reconstruct it. Mobile-phone mapping produced open network data for Nairobi's matatus~\cite{williams2015_digital_matatu}, and collaborative mapping in Nairobi and Maputo has made minibus systems visible in planning~\cite{klopp2019_mapping_minibuses}. In South Africa, more than 500 Cape Town routes have been clustered into trunk, intermediate and feeder routes~\cite{dupreez2019_paratransit_route_typology}, and GPS traces from Tshwane have been extrapolated to minibus-taxi trip counts per street segment~\cite{debeer2025_quantifying_paratransit}, while traces from Kampala reveal how drivers search for passengers~\cite{ndibatya2021_characterising_mbt_movement}. This work builds on general methods for stop detection and trajectory mining~\cite{zheng2015_trajectory_data_mining}, and its units of analysis are routes, corridors and road segments. Vehicles have been grouped by home location~\cite{pretorius2024_uneven_transitions}, but not by the hubs and routes that they share, which is the unit at which a charging pilot would be planned.

\subsection{Comparing models of different structure}
\label{sec:rw_comparison}
% Discuss > P1: Docking and alignment -> An established practice in agent-based modelling, usually aimed at showing that two models agree on common outputs. In eMBT work, two energy-simulation tools have been reconciled. Replay and ABM do not share an output, and multi-fidelity methods assume they do. Model choice is not neutral: it fixes which outcomes planners can see. This motivates model-native selection followed by cross-evaluation.
% CHANGE: Last sentence points to Section 4.1 for why only the agent-based model has ICE competitors (supervisor R2 #45: expected the explanation here); it introduces the term "ICE competitors" (R2 self-review #14).

Comparing models of different structure is an established practice in agent-based modelling. Docking tests whether two models of the same system produce equivalent results, at the level of numerical, distributional or relational equivalence~\cite{axtell1996_aligning_simulation_models}. Replication studies show that independently built models of the same system can disagree for reasons of implementation, and they define criteria for when two models match~\cite{edmonds2003_replication_alignment, wilensky2007_making_models_match}. Independent replication can expose artefacts that a single implementation hides~\cite{collins2024_abm_validation_methods}. In minibus-taxi electrification, the alignment of two energy-simulation tools reconciled consumption estimates that differed by a factor of more than two~\cite{abraham2023_aligned_embt_simulation}. These comparisons ask whether models agree on a common output. Data replay and agent-based simulation do not share one: replay reports whether a vehicle-day strands, whereas the agent-based model, for this study, focuses on how many passengers an EV carries. The choice of model therefore shapes which outcomes planners can see~\cite{saltelli2020_models_serve_society, booysen2025_sys_of_sys_em_planning, sello2026_planning_paradigms}. A fair comparison lets each model select a design by its own measures and then evaluates both designs in the model that can represent the outcomes of both. Only the agent-based model includes ICE competitors, the ICE taxis that share the EVs' routes; Section~\ref{sec:methodology_design} explains why and how the designs are still compared like-for-like.

\subsection{Minibus-taxi operation in Cape Town}

% CHANGE: "route associations" -> "taxi associations", as elsewhere in the paper; hold-back described as stopping to wait for more passengers (R2 self-review #15).
Minibus taxis carry the largest share of the public-transport trips in Cape Town~\cite{statssa2022_nhts, coct2023_citp}. Their operation is shaped by vehicle owners, taxi associations and the provincial operating-licence system~\cite{behrens2015_paratransit, vegter2020_sa_mbt_industry}. Five operating characteristics are particularly relevant to electrification and to the models developed in this study.

First, passengers commonly board at taxi ranks, where vehicles queue on a first-in, first-out basis and depart when sufficiently full~\cite{behrens2015_paratransit}. This is known as ``fill-and-go'' loading. Second, passengers may board or alight at stops along a route, although boarding is generally concentrated at terminals. Third, vehicles tend to operate on a limited set of routes between established terminals~\cite{coct2021_taxiroutes, dupreez2019_paratransit_route_typology}. Fourth, drivers may stop and wait for more passengers, or move slowly along a route to find them, a practice referred to here as ``hold-back''~\cite{ndibatya2021_characterising_mbt_movement}. Finally, demand is directional and varies over the day. During the morning peak, many passengers travel from residential areas towards employment centres, leaving some taxis with relatively few passengers on the return journey~\cite{coct2023_citp}. The pattern often reverses in the evening.

These features create specific charging constraints. Long dwell periods at ranks and terminals during the midday off-peak hours provide potential charging opportunities, but a taxi that leaves a loading queue to charge may lose its position and the passengers it would otherwise have carried. The models therefore need to represent both the availability of charging opportunities and the cost of using them.

Shown in Figure~\ref{fig:study_context}a, the study area is the union of the City of Cape Town metropolitan municipality and the surrounding Stellenbosch and Drakenstein municipalities. Telemetry data outside the area is flagged but not deleted. Around 19\% of the driven distance lies outside the study area, and a vehicle-day with 50~km or more outside the area is considered a long-distance day.

\subsection{Vehicle telemetry}
\label{sec:telemetry}

The observed fleet dataset is a subset of the Sebenza router telemetry~\cite{sebenza2025_telemetry}, which is a GPS dataset of minibus taxis in South Africa. The subset consisted of 378 minibus taxis operating in Cape Town over 11 weekdays from 1 to 15 December 2025, resulting in a total of 3,916 vehicle-days. The festive period from 16 December was excluded, because it produces a distinct mobility regime~\cite{sello2026_gau_mbt_distance}. Each GPS point includes a pseudonymised vehicle identifier, a timestamp, a position, and a quality flag. The median sampling interval is 79 seconds, with the 5th to 95th percentile ranging from 60 to 81 seconds. On average, a moving taxi covers a median distance of 435 meters between two points. While the start, end, and overnight positions of each day are observed, stops shorter than 2 to 3 minutes, which include most boarding and alighting events along a route, are not captured in the dataset.

Table~\ref{tab:cpt_fleet_summary} summarises the duty cycle of the observed Cape Town fleet. The median valid vehicle-day covers 190~km (interquartile range 130 to 247~km), whereas the battery window of the study EV gives about 130~km; only 24.9\% of the valid days fall within this range. The median driving window, from the departure after the first stop of the day to the arrival at the last, is 17.4~h. The median taxi therefore parks for about 6.6~h, in which a 3.7~kW socket adds at most about 22~kWh, less than a third of the median daily need of 72~kWh. Further analysis shows that long dwells concentrate at ranks, official route termini, and stations. Additionally, each vehicle varies strongly from day to day: the intraclass correlation is 0.43 (95\% confidence interval 0.36 to 0.50) for the daily distance and 0.62 (0.56 to 0.67) for the daily moving time, thus 57\% of the variance of the daily distance lies between the days of one vehicle.

\subsection{Observation period, data quality and analysis sample}
\label{sec:study_days}
Established by \citet{pretorius2024_grid_mobility_interdependence}, an operational vehicle-day runs from 04:00 to 03:59 the following morning. This boundary groups overnight charging with the operating cycle that precedes it, since most taxis are parked during this period. A GPS step is treated as evidence of data loss, and the vehicle-day is marked invalid, when consecutive observations are more than 600 seconds apart and more than 1,000~m apart. A long interval without corresponding movement is instead treated as a parked period.

Of the 3,916 observed vehicle-days, 2,446 (62.5\%) satisfy the validity criteria. The valid share varies from 29\% to 87\% across dates, while the number of active vehicles remains comparatively stable at 351--360 per day. This pattern suggests that variation in the valid share reflects data completeness more than changes in fleet activity. The agent-based model therefore applies a day-level correction factor when scaling observed activity to daily operation.

The study days fall into two classes (Table~\ref{tab:cpt_fleet_summary}). The eight term weekdays (1 to 5 and 8 to 10 December) end with the last school day, and the three school-holiday weekdays (11, 12 and 15 December) follow it~\cite{dbe2023_school_calendar}. The holiday weekdays have three times the share of long-distance days (7.5\% against 2.5\%) and a longer mean distance (214 against 193~km), whereas without the long-distance days the two classes are almost the same (mean 184 against 185~km). They therefore represent a different demand regime and are excluded from every fit; only the structural stages (stop events, network, communities and routes) use them, and the calibration and the replay use the term weekdays. Of the term weekdays, Tuesday 2 and Wednesday 10 December, with valid shares of 79\% and 86\%, are the hold-out days, and the other six are the calibration days. Section~\ref{sec:methodology_design} gives the steps that use each class. No candidate community is excluded on data quality. Their valid shares are 59\% to 75\%, their gap times 1\% to 2\%, and their shares of driving outside the network 14\% to 47\%.

\subsection{Comparative design and shared assumptions}
\label{sec:methodology_design}

The study evaluates the same observed vehicles using two models, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}. In the data-replay model (\(\MR\)), each vehicle is replayed as an observed EV along its recorded movements. In the agent-based model (\(\MA\)), drivers generate realistic movements through operational decisions and can respond to energy and charging constraints.

In \(\MA\), the EV agents compete for passengers with ICE competitors: synthetic agents that follow the same decision model and operate on the same routes (Section~\ref{sec:demand}). \(\MR\) has no ICE competitors, for two reasons. First, replayed movement is fixed and passengers are not represented, so other vehicles could not change any outcome of \(\MR\). Second, in \(\MA\) the competitors are what make charging costly: while an EV charges, they can carry the passengers that it would have carried. The designs are still compared like-for-like, because \(\xR\) and \(\xA\) are both evaluated in \(\MA\) against the same ICE competitors (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

Where their operating periods differ, the evaluation windows are made as comparable as the model structures allow. One asymmetry remains: \(\MR\) allows vehicles to charge during recorded dwell periods that include waiting in a rank loading queue, whereas \(\MA\) requires drivers to leave the queue to charge. This assumption favours \(\MR\) and should be considered when interpreting the results.

\subsubsection{Vehicle and charging values}
\label{subsubsec:vehicle_assumptions}
Both models use one assumption set based on the Higer H5C, a 15-seat electric minibus~\cite{higer_h5c_ev_2022}. The vehicle has a usable battery capacity (\( C \)) of 70.47~kWh, an empirically measured energy consumption rate of 0.38~kWh/km in loaded operation~\cite{sello2026_ekamva_performance}, a vehicle charging limit of 60~kW and a charging efficiency of 90\%. The battery operates between a reserve of \( 0.2\,C \) and a charge limit of \( 0.9\,C \). This window holds 49.3~kWh, or about 130~km. Every vehicle charges overnight at 3.7~kW from a socket outlet, and site chargers serve vehicles in a first-come, first-served queue.

\subsubsection{Unit of analysis and study days}
\label{subsubsec:unit_of_analysis}

The unit of analysis is the vehicle community: a group of vehicles with overlapping operating areas, routes and hubs. These shared operating patterns create overlapping charging requirements and make communities a useful scale for evaluating electrification pilots. A community is a data-derived operating group, not a taxi association, fleet operator or licensed route.

Network construction, route inference and community detection use all 11 observation weekdays. Agent-based model calibration uses the six designated calibration days, while the two hold-out days remain unused until the calibrated model has been accepted. Since \(\MR\) has no fitted behavioural parameters, it replays all eight term weekdays.

\subsubsection{Planning principle}
The study selects one charging design per model and community. The selection principle is to achieve service performance close to the best observed within the model's feasible design set while minimising the required infrastructure. Installed power, calculated as the number of connectors multiplied by their rated power, serves as a proxy for infrastructure cost during the selection; the selected designs are then priced with a cost model (Section~\ref{sec:synthesis}).

% CHANGE: M_R selects on the steady-state not-stranded share, not on the viable share of Pretorius et al., and the reason is given (R2 self-review #2: "viable" is used only in the sense of Pretorius et al.).
The service measure depends on the model. In \(\MR\), the selection rule uses the share of vehicle-days that do not strand under repeated operation, in the steady state described by \citet{pretorius2024_grid_mobility_interdependence}. It does not use their viable share, which also counts a day below the \(0.2\,C\) reserve as a failure, because in \(\MA\) a level below the reserve is normal behaviour: drivers look for a charger at that level. Both models therefore select by the same failure, stranding. In \(\MA\), it uses the passenger throughput of the EV agents, alongside constraints on expired passenger requests. The measures are defined below, and the precise selection rules are given in Section~\ref{sec:selection}. Since the models represent different aspects of operation, the selected designs are subsequently evaluated in a common agent-based framework, and each selected design is also evaluated in the replay model.

The EV passenger change is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_{\mathrm{EV}} = \frac{M_{\mathrm{EV}}^{\text{EV run}}}{M_{\mathrm{EV}}^{\text{ICE run}}} - 1,
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Stop events and the operational network}
\label{sec:network}

Stop events are extracted from the GPS trajectories with an anchored-dwell algorithm that tolerates isolated positioning errors. An event has at least three inlier points within 30~m of its anchor and lasts at least 180~s. At the sampling interval of about 80~s, stops shorter than 2 to 3~min, which include most boarding and alighting along a route, are not observable. The home of a vehicle is the location within 300~m of which the first and last points of at least 75\% of its valid vehicle-days lie.

One operational network is inferred and applied to all communities. Its nodes come from evidence of stopping where grid cells with stop events from at least five vehicle-days and three vehicles, merged by complete linkage within 200~m, together with the formal ranks, termini and stations that the fleet uses. Its directed edges come from evidence of driving which constitute hops between nodes on at least two vehicle-days, their reverse edges, and links between nodes within 300~m. Edge lengths are shortest drivable paths on OpenStreetMap. After one manual review of 211 flagged items, the network has 726 nodes, of which 18 are ranks. It captures 76\% of the stop events and represents 66.5\% of the distance driven between stops with respect to the full fleet. 

\subsubsection{Route library and driver route sets}
\label{sec:routes}
% TODO: Add a reference for the Jaccard distance.
% CHANGE: "becomes an agent of both models" -> defines the qualified vehicles, with their numbers (supervisor R2 #50: unclear which vehicles both models electrify; "agent" does not apply to M_R).
Typically, minibus taxis load at a terminal and depart when full~\cite{behrens2015_paratransit}. A trip therefore runs between two detected anchor dwells, consisting of stop events of 10~min or more at non-home nodes. The trips of each terminal pair are grouped by the Jaccard similarity of their node sets, using a threshold of 0.5. Each group with trips on at least three vehicle-days and two dates becomes a route, represented by its medoid trip. The fleet library holds 586 observed and 503 derived reverse routes. To limit the agent's decision space, the eligible routes of a driver are the smallest set of routes that covers 80\% of its trip distance on qualified routes, together with their reverse routes. Routes shorter than 5~km in the network are excluded. A vehicle with at least one eligible route qualifies for the models: both models electrify it, and it becomes an EV agent in \MA. In the selected communities, 89 of the 101 vehicles qualify in Khayelitsha, all 44 in Bellville and 20 of the 26 in Wynberg (Table~\ref{tab:models}). In \MA, a route is one discrete unit of operation, where a driver cannot charge at a rank part-way along it.

\subsubsection{Vehicle communities and pilot selection}
\label{sec:communities}

Vehicle communities are identified from four views of observed operation: dwell time at network nodes, dwell time at formal ranks, use of network edges, and origin--destination pairs of anchor dwells. Features are weighted using term frequency--inverse document frequency (TF--IDF). The similarity between two vehicles is calculated as the mean cosine similarity across the views available for both vehicles.

Each vehicle is connected to its 15 nearest neighbours in a similarity graph. The Leiden algorithm partitions this graph across a range of resolution parameters, using 50 random seeds at each resolution. The seed-level partitions are combined into a consensus partition. A resolution of 0.75 is selected, producing eight communities while providing the best stability among the resolutions that retain at least 90\% of corridor use within a single community.

% CHANGE: V_3 is hub charging only, not "depot only" charging; "binding" defined and used for the screening condition (R2 self-review #12).
Candidate charging sites are identified from each community's most frequently used non-home nodes. Nodes are ranked by the number of community vehicles with dwell periods of at least 20 minutes, and the first three are retained as candidate sites in both models. Each community is then screened using the energy core of \(\MR\) under two charging scenarios: overnight charging only, and overnight charging plus unrestricted 60 kW charging at the three candidate hubs. The corresponding viable shares of vehicle-days are denoted by \(V_1\) and \(V_2\). The binding of hub charging, \(B = V_2 - V_1\), is the gain in the viable share when hub charging is added to overnight charging. A large binding means that overnight charging alone leaves many vehicle-days non-viable, so the community needs hub chargers. For each candidate hub, the estimated connector requirement is based on the 95th percentile of the observed daily peak number of simultaneous charging sessions in the second scenario. This screening rule was established to avoid biasing the outcomes of the data-replay agent-based comparison.

A community qualifies for pilot selection when it contains at least 15 vehicles, has a formal main hub, has a binding of hub charging of at least 15 percentage points (\(B \geq 15\)), and has eligible routes that represent at least 30\% of the median driver's trip distance. The last condition ensures that the agent-based model represents a meaningful share of community operation. The three qualifying communities with the highest \(V_2\) values are selected, subject to having distinct main hubs. Consequently, the results apply most directly to communities that meet these electrification-readiness criteria, rather than to all minibus-taxi communities. 

\subsection{Fixed-mobility data-replay}
\label{sec:replay}

% CHANGE: The replayed vehicle-days are the valid term-weekday days that are not long-distance days (R2 self-review #9: explains the lower home energy of M_R). Viable defined as in Pretorius et al., and conclusive defined (R2 self-review #2, #15).
The data-replay model (\(\MR\)) follows the trajectory-replay approach used in earlier minibus-taxi electrification studies~\cite{pretorius2024_grid_mobility_interdependence,booysen2026_cpt_embt_transition}. Each valid vehicle-day of the qualified vehicles on the term weekdays that is not a long-distance day is replayed using the energy-consumption and battery assumptions of the study EV~\cite{sello2026_ekamva_performance, higer_h5c_ev_2022}. Trajectories, departure times and dwell periods remain fixed. A vehicle can charge when it is parked near an available charger but cannot divert from its observed movement to reach one.

A charging opportunity is defined as a stop lasting at least 10 minutes within 100~m of a candidate charging site. Vehicles from each date, called a fleet day, are replayed in chronological order, sharing the available connectors. A vehicle whose battery is below \(0.9C\) joins the first-in, first-out queue and charges at the connector power (\( P \)) of the design and charger efficiency (\( \eta \)) until either its battery reaches \(0.9C\) or the observed stop ends. Since the replay uses observed dwell periods, a vehicle can charge during time spent waiting in a rank loading queue. This assumes that charging connectors are available at the loading bays. The agent-based model does not permit this as a driver must leave the loading queue to travel to a charging station. This asymmetry is retained in the comparison and favours the replay model. Additionally, every vehicle can also charge overnight, subject to the available parked time and the upper battery limit. The energy added overnight is

\begin{equation}
    E^{\text{night}} = \min\left(0.9\,C - E^{\text{end}},\ \eta P_o\, \Delta t_{\text{park}}\right), \qquad \Delta t_{\text{park}} = t^{\text{start}} + 24\,\text{h} - t^{\text{end}},
    \label{eq:overnight}
\end{equation}

\noindent where \( P_o = 3.7 \)~kW is the overnight charging power, \( E^{\text{end}} \) is the battery energy at the end of driving, and \( t^{\text{start}} \) and \( t^{\text{end}} \) are the start and end of the driving window.

Following \citet{pretorius2024_grid_mobility_interdependence}, a vehicle-day is viable when the vehicle can repeat its duty cycle in the steady state without its battery falling below the \(0.2\,C\) reserve. Each date is therefore simulated repeatedly, starting at \(0.9C\), until the daily starting battery levels converge to a fixed point or a sustainable cycle within a tolerance of \(0.001C\), all active vehicles have been resolved, or 100 iterations have been completed~\cite{booysen2026_cpt_embt_transition}. A vehicle that reaches zero battery energy strands and leaves the active set. Each vehicle-day is classified as viable, below reserve, stranded or unconverged. A below-reserve or stranded day is non-viable, and a day that is not unconverged is conclusive. The stranded share is also reported for \(\MA\), where stranding is treated as a failure condition, providing a common reliability measure for the comparison.

\subsection{Decision-adaptive agent-based model}
\label{sec:abm}
% CHANGE: "Each observed vehicle" -> "Each qualified vehicle", and the term EV agent introduced (supervisor R2 #50, #52).
The agent-based model (\(\MA\)) is implemented using the discrete-event simulation framework established by \citet{sello2025_stellenbosch_embt_abm}. Each qualified vehicle is represented by a driver agent, called an EV agent once it is electrified, who decides when to work, which route to operate, how long to wait for passengers, when to idle and, after electrification, when to charge. Unlike the replay model, \(\MA\) generates vehicle movement from these decisions.

Each simulated day represents a typical term weekday on the inferred operational network. The model specification follows the Overview, Design concepts and Details (ODD) protocol~\cite{grimm2010_odd_protocol,grimm2020_odd_second_update}, with the decision-making questions of its ODD+D extension~\cite{muller2013_odd_d}, and the full description is provided in \ref{app:abm}. The following subsections focus on the mechanisms relevant to the comparison and on changes made relative to the earlier model.

\subsubsection{Operation}

When a driver is not servicing a route, the agent chooses between its eligible routes and idling using a multinomial logit model~\cite{mcfadden1974_mnl}. Route utility depends on the requests waiting at the current node, the number of competing drivers, and the driver's memory of route-level demand. This memory records the number of passengers served and their passenger-distance contribution during each 30-minute interval of the day. Idling becomes more attractive during intervals that the driver remembers as having low demand.

% CHANGE: The stop decision is linked to hold-back (R2 self-review #15: the term was defined in Section 3.1 but not used).
At each stop, the driver decides whether to continue waiting for more passengers, the hold-back of Section~\ref{sec:study_area}, according to
\begin{equation}
    \pi = \left[ 1 + \exp\left(-\left(b + w_d\, d - w_o\, o\right)\right) \right]^{-1},
\end{equation}

\noindent where \(d\) is the remembered demand for the route, scaled by vehicle capacity, and \(o\) is the current onboard occupancy as a share of capacity. The parameters \(b\), \(w_d\) and \(w_o\) are calibrated. The initial stop of a route represents fill-and-go loading at a rank and has separate parameters, allowing drivers to wait according to their remembered demand at the terminal.

Relative to the earlier model by \citet{sello2025_stellenbosch_embt_abm}, the stop decision now uses route-specific demand memory, rank loading is represented explicitly, and includes a last-run rule that prevents a driver from starting a route that would extend beyond its shift. A passenger boards only if the vehicle is at the request's origin, has an available seat and serves the passenger's destination. Unserviced requests expire after two hours. Travel times are drawn from windowed log-normal distributions fitted to observed network hops, while each agent's shift is based on its observed first and last movements.

\subsubsection{Charging}
% CHANGE: "the observed agents become EVs, while their synthetic ICE copies remain" -> EV agents and ICE competitors (supervisor R2 #52: consistent terms).

During the electrification experiments, the EV agents are electrified, while their ICE competitors remain internal combustion vehicles. Each EV driver follows a demand-aware charging policy. The vehicle always charges when its state of charge falls below \(s_{\min}=0.2\), never charges above \(s_{\max}=0.9\), and charges between these limits when
% TODO: Make sure the notation is consistent with other papers.
\begin{equation}
    \frac{E(t)}{C} < s_{\min} + \left(s_{\max} - s_{\min}\right)\left(1 - \hat{h}_t\right),
\end{equation}

\noindent where \(E(t)\) is the battery energy and \(\hat{h}_t\in[0,1]\) is the normalised demand remembered by the driver for the current time interval. The policy makes charging more attractive when the driver expects charging to incur a smaller passenger-service opportunity cost.

Drivers can initiate charging only when they are not on a route. They travel to the nearest charging station and join its first-in, first-out queue. A driver waiting to load at a rank must leave the queue to charge and subsequently return to passenger service. Reserve rules reduce avoidable stranding: a driver may start a route only when the battery can support the route, the subsequent drive to the nearest station and the reserve of \(0.2C\). The reserve is the minimum level \(s_{\min}\) of the charging policy times the capacity, so a change of \(s_{\min}\) also changes the reserve. A route is not eligible if a full battery cannot support its completion. While a vehicle is at home, it can charge at 3.7~kW. If an EV strands, it loses its onboard passengers and restarts from home with a full battery on the following day.

\subsubsection{Run horizon}
A cold simulation begins with empty driver memories and includes a warm-up period of 49--91 days. The warm-up ends when the seven-day means of passenger-service and route-choice measures remain close to their long-run means for three random seeds. The accepted warm-up is stored and reused for each experiment within a community. Each experiment begins from this saved warm start and runs for 14 settling days, allowing EV drivers to learn their charging behaviour. These are followed by 14 evaluation days, which provide the outcomes used for design selection and comparison.

\subsection{Mixed-fleet population and passenger demand}
\label{sec:demand}

% CHANGE: Synthetic ICE copies called ICE competitors and stated to follow the same decision model; "a larger corridor population" -> "all the drivers on these routes" (supervisor R2 #49: make explicit that they are agents; #52: consistent terms; #4: "corridor").
The GPS dataset covers only a subset of the taxis operating from each hub --- those fitted with Sebenza Wi-Fi routers. Thus, to represent all the drivers on these routes, \(\MA\) adds ICE competitors: synthetic copies of the EV agents that follow the same decision model. The resulting population is, on average, \(k\) times the number of EV agents. Each copy follows its associated agent's eligible routes, idle locations and shift pattern, but is assigned a nearby home location and a shift adjusted by a random draw with a standard deviation of 30 minutes. This construction assumes that the EV agents provide a useful representation of the wider population operating from the same hub. The EV agents account for 2.6\%--6.6\% of the simulated driver population (Table~\ref{tab:models}), reflecting the small share expected in an initial pilot. The ICE competitors retain ICE drivetrains and compete with the EVs for passenger requests.

Since GPS trajectories do not reveal passenger demand directly, the model generates a latent demand process using observed operating patterns and published passenger counts. The observed departures, routes and stops determine the structure, direction and timing of demand, while daily boardings at the main hub in the CITP~\cite{coct2023_citp} determine its overall scale. Each departure has a load factor of 1, except for counter-peak travel during the morning and afternoon peaks. For those departures, the load factor is set to the counter-peak-to-peak ratio of vehicle trips in CITP Table 3-18, which ranges from 0.09 to 0.39. The demand scale factor is

\begin{equation}
    k = \frac{B}{Q\,L},
\end{equation}

\noindent where \(B\) is the published daily number of boardings at the main hub, \(Q\) is vehicle capacity and \(L\) is the estimated number of full agent loads from the hub per calibration day.

% CHANGE: "the agents" -> "the EV agents" here, and "the observed agent population" -> "the qualified vehicles" below (supervisor R2 #52: consistent terms).
A share of 0.8 of passengers board at the route origin and alight downstream in proportion to the observed stop-event distribution. The remaining passengers travel between intermediate route nodes. Demand is generated only for origin--destination pairs that are served by at least one eligible route, follows the hourly operating profile of the EV agents and is sampled as a non-homogeneous Poisson process. This construction makes the simulated terminal boardings at the main hub equal the published boarding total by design.

The scale factor \(k\) is checked against an upper bound inferred from the provincial operating-licence register~\cite{wcpp2022_transport_public_works}. The Bellville estimate exceeds this bound, indicating uncertainty in the relationship between the qualified vehicles and the full hub population. This limitation is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion_limitations}; it should not be interpreted as evidence that the synthetic population is an exact reconstruction of the entire taxi service.

\subsection{Calibration and validation of the agent-based model}
\label{sec:calibration}
% CHANGE: Says what validation means in this section and how it differs from a validated design in the design experiment (R2 self-review #11). TPE is defined in Section 4.1 (R2 self-review #16).

Calibration fits the behavioural parameters of \(\MA\) to the observed movement. Validation then checks the calibrated model against what the fit did not use: cold runs on new seeds, the two hold-out days and the quantities of the replay rules (Section~\ref{sec:representation}). In the design experiment, a validated design has a different meaning: a search candidate that is evaluated again on the selection seeds (Section~\ref{sec:selection}).

\subsubsection{Calibration}

The ICE baseline of each community is calibrated to reproduce observed operating patterns that determine an EV's daily energy requirement and opportunities for charging. Calibration targets include the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of daily distance, daily moving time and each agent's day-to-day coefficient of variation in daily distance. The hourly share of agents moving is also assessed, but is used as a realism check rather than a target that must be matched hour by hour. Additionally, the model must serve at least 95\% of passenger requests, and median seat occupancy should approach 0.6. These are plausibility constraints and goals rather than observed calibration targets. The fit of each target is expressed as its absolute error relative to the target's estimated sampling variability, calculated from 500 bootstrap resamples of complete calibration dates.

Following \citet{thiele2014_abm_parameter_estimation}, calibration proceeds in two stages. Hand tuning first identifies a plausible region of parameter space and reveals structural gaps that cannot be resolved by parameter adjustment alone. In Khayelitsha, these gaps required a travel-time scaling adjustment and the introduction of the last-run rule. A predefined search strategy then optimises nine behavioural parameters using a constrained, two-objective TPE (Section~\ref{sec:objectives}). Selected configurations are validated with cold runs, which start from empty driver memories and run the full warm-up, on three new random seeds.

In Khayelitsha the search started from the calibrated values of Wynberg, and no validated search trial fitted the daily distance and moving time better than this start point, so the start point was accepted (\ref{app:calibration}). Finally, the accepted configuration is assessed against the held-out observation days. 

\subsubsection{Fidelity of the simulated movement}
\label{sec:representation}

The movement generated by \(\MA\) differs from the observed movement as agents operate on a simplified network, choose from a limited set of inferred routes and stop only at network nodes. Differences between the selected charging designs could therefore arise from the representation of movement, rather than from driver adaptation alone. To assess this effect, the data-replay rules are applied to the simulated movements from five validated ICE runs of \(\MA\). Each simulated home-to-home duty is treated as one vehicle-day. Distance is calculated from network travel, charging opportunities are identified from simulated dwell periods at candidate sites, and overnight energy is calculated using the same rule as in Equation~\eqref{eq:overnight}. The first eight evaluation days of each seed are used to match the number of days replayed in \(\MR\), and outcomes are averaged across the five seeds.

The resulting charging design, \(\xS\), is the design selected by the \(\MR\) rule when applied to simulated rather than observed movements. Comparing \(\xR\) with \(\xS\) isolates the effect of the movement representation under the replay selection rule. Comparing \(\xS\) with \(\xA\) captures driver adaptation together with the differences between the models' service measures and selection rules. The difference in EV passenger change between \(\xR\) and \(\xA\) is decomposed as

\begin{equation}
    \underbrace{\bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}}(\xR) - \bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}}(\xA)}_{\text{total}}
    = \underbrace{\bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}}(\xR) - \bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}}(\xS)}_{\text{representation}}
    + \underbrace{\bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}}(\xS) - \bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}}(\xA)}_{\text{behaviour and selection}},
\end{equation}

\noindent where \( \bar{\Delta}_{\mathrm{EV}} \) is the mean EV passenger change. The fidelity of the movement is reported with the quantities that the replay rules use: the daily distance, the overnight time, the charging opportunities at the sites and the ceiling of the not-stranded share. No pass threshold is set.


\subsection{Charging design experiment}
\label{sec:experiment}

\subsubsection{Candidate sites and design space}
\label{sec:design_space}

Each charging design is represented by

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x} = (N_1, N_2, N_3, P) \in \mathcal{X}, \qquad N_j \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 2\hat{N}_j\}, \qquad P \in \{22, 50\}\ \text{kW},
\end{equation}

\noindent where \(N_j\) is the number of connectors installed at candidate hub \(j\), and \(P\) is the rated power of each connector. Each hub can have between zero and \(2\hat{N}_j\) connectors, where \(\hat{N}_j\) is the connector estimate derived during screening. Connector power is either 22 or 50 kW. A value of \(N_j=0\) means that no charging site is installed at hub \(j\). Site selection is therefore part of the optimisation. The resulting design space \(\mathcal{X}\) contains 2,645 candidates in Khayelitsha, 1,169 in Bellville and 349 in Wynberg. Table~\ref{tab:hubs} gives \(\hat{N}_j\) for each hub. To test whether the bound constrains the selection, three designs above it, up to \(3\hat{N}_j\), are also evaluated in \(\MA\) in Khayelitsha, the community with the largest selected designs.

\subsubsection{Selection rules}
\label{sec:selection}
% CHANGE: The measures, Table 5 and the definitions of the EV passenger change and loss moved to Section 4.1 (supervisor R2 #55); this subsection keeps the selection rules.

Each model selects its design with the measures of Section~\ref{sec:objectives}.

% CHANGE: The ceiling is the highest not-stranded share of the enumerated designs; the 99-connector design only checks that the design space reaches it (R2 self-review #6: the text described the check as the ceiling). "viable" -> "not stranded" (#2). The 40 trials evaluate 36 to 39 distinct designs (#7), and "validated design" is defined (#11).
The replay model is deterministic, allowing every candidate design to be evaluated. Its Pareto front is therefore exact within the specified design space. Because replayed vehicles cannot divert to a charging station, even unlimited connectors cannot prevent every vehicle-day from stranding. The ceiling is therefore the highest not-stranded share of the enumerated designs, which is the service of the best design. A design with 99 connectors at each candidate site confirms that the design space reaches this ceiling. The replay candidates must reach at least 95\% of the ceiling. Among those candidates, \(\xR\) is selected by minimising peak site load and then installed power. The selected design is used as the common reference for the first research question.

The agent-based model is more computationally expensive, so it cannot feasibly evaluate every candidate design. Instead, a multi-objective TPE search proposes 40 trials using one seed per trial, starting from \(\xR\). A design that the search proposes again reuses its first result, so the 40 trials evaluate 36 to 39 distinct designs (Table~\ref{tab:computational_cost}). The search minimises expired passenger requests, mean passenger waiting time and peak site load, while maximising the passengers carried by the EV agents. The resulting candidates and \(\xR\) are then evaluated on five new seeds, the selection seeds, with their paired ICE counterfactuals; a design evaluated on these seeds is called a validated design. Unlike the replay front, the agent-based front is therefore an estimate. The final agent-based design, \(\xA\), is chosen with the relative tolerance of the replay rule. With \(S = 1 + \Delta_{\mathrm{EV}}\), a candidate must keep at least 95\% of the EV passengers of the best validated design, \(S \ge 0.95\,S_{\text{best}}\), and its mean expired-request share must be within one percentage point of the minimum. Among the candidates, the selected design has the lowest peak site load and then the lowest installed power. No design is selected manually. The two models therefore use the same form of tolerance. The selection is also reported for tolerances of 90\%, 97.5\% and 99\% (\ref{app:supplementary}). A validated EV run that strands invalidates the selection; this condition applies to the selection runs only.


\subsubsection{Cross-evaluation, sensitivity and synthesis}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
\label{sec:synthesis}

The consequences of selecting infrastructure under fixed-mobility assumptions are measured by comparing the outcomes of \(\xR\) and \(\xA\) in \(\MA\) on ten seeds that the search and the selection did not use. On the selection seeds, part of the lead of \(\xA\) is seed noise, so a comparison on these seeds would favour \(\xA\). For each outcome, the paired difference is calculated between the two designs on each seed and then averaged. The standard error of the mean paired difference quantifies the uncertainty across the ten seeds. Since both designs use the same seed and warm start, the paired comparison reduces variation arising from different random simulation conditions. Differences within two standard errors of zero are treated as indistinguishable at the resolution of this experiment.

% CHANGE: "the ICE vehicles take" -> "the ICE competitors take" (supervisor R2 #52: consistent terms). Says when the capture share can exceed 1 (R2 self-review #20).
Changes in expired requests are also examined. The share of the passengers that the EV agents lose and the ICE competitors take is measured directly. For each EV run and the ICE run of the same seed, the capture share is the change of the passengers of all other drivers (gained) divided by the passengers that the EV agents lose (lost). It can exceed 1 when the other drivers gain more passengers than the EV agents lose, through seed variation or because fewer requests expire.

Robustness is assessed by evaluating \(\xR\) and \(\xA\), without reselecting either design, under six alternative conditions: energy consumption 15\% below and above the baseline; charging-policy minimums of 0.1 and 0.35; and fleet scale factors \(k\) equal to 0.5 and 1.5 times their baseline values. Each case is evaluated on five fresh seeds, with a corresponding ICE counterfactual where the changed condition affects ICE operation.

% CHANGE: Defines the cost of x_R and its sign against the paired differences in the tables (R2 self-review #5: the text gave it as positive, the tables as negative, and it was defined only in the Discussion).
For each community, the analysis records three results: the difference in installed power between \(\xA\) and \(\xR\), the cost of \(\xR\), and the EV passenger change achieved by \(\xA\) relative to the ICE counterfactual. The cost of \(\xR\) is the number of percentage points by which the EV passenger change of \(\xR\) falls short of that of \(\xA\) when both are evaluated in \(\MA\). The tables report the paired difference, \(\xR\) minus \(\xA\), which is the negative of this cost. Because each model selects its design in its own objective space, the direction of the cross-evaluation in \(\MA\) is partly built into the method. The analysis therefore reports the size of each difference and its form: whether the agent-based design adds capacity, moves connectors between sites, or both. A result is robust when its size and form persist across the tested cases. These experiments assess the robustness of the selected designs' outcomes. They do not test whether the designs themselves would remain optimal under the alternative conditions.

To relate the passenger result to an operator's decision, each design is also priced (Table~\ref{tab:cost_model}). The annual cost is the installed connector cost, annualised over ten years, plus the electricity demand charges of each site. A 50~kW DC connector costs the hardware and the installation cost of a US estimate for its number of chargers at the site~\cite{nicholas2019_icct_charging_costs}, corrected from 2019 to December 2025 with the US producer and consumer price indices and converted at the December 2025 exchange rate. The demand charges are those of the City of Cape Town low-voltage time-of-use tariff of 2025/26~\cite{coct2025_electricity_tariffs}: a charge on the measured peak of each site and a network capacity charge on its installed power, at a power factor of 0.96. The revenue is the fare of the additional passengers that the EV agents carry, on 250 term weekdays a year. Because the fare of the studied routes is not published, the result is the break-even fare at which the additional fare revenue pays the additional cost of \(\xA\) against \(\xR\). The discount rate (0, 4, 8 and 12\% real) and the operating days (250 and 300) are varied.

\subsection{Electrification outcomes under a common design (RQ1)}
\label{sec:results_rq1}

Under the reference design \(\xR\), the two models predict substantially different electrification outcomes (Table~\ref{tab:reference_design}). In the data-replay model, 57.0\%, 22.6\% and 49.0\% of vehicle-days strand in Khayelitsha, Bellville, and Wynberg, respectively. These failures largely reflect the mismatch between daily energy requirements and the charging opportunities available along the fixed trajectories. Median daily energy needs are 79, 55 and 70 kWh, whereas overnight charging at 3.7 kW supplies approximately 23, 19 and 19 kWh. \subsection{Selected charging infrastructure (RQ2)}
\label{sec:results_rq2}
% CHANGE: The two peaks of x_A in Khayelitsha are tied to their seeds, 600 kW on the fresh seeds and 580 kW on the selection seeds (R2 self-review #4). Removed the one-standard-error rule, which round 1 dropped from the method (#1). The bound check names the best validated design and why its bound matters (#8).

The models select different charging designs in all three communities (Table~\ref{tab:two_by_two} and Figure~\ref{fig:design_space}). The difference has two forms. In Khayelitsha, the agent-based model selects more capacity: 650~kW of installed power (5, 6 and 2 connectors at 50~kW; peak site load 600~kW on the ten fresh seeds), against 250~kW in the replay model. In Bellville and Wynberg, it selects the same installed power as the replay model (200~kW and 100~kW) at different hubs. In Bellville it puts all four connectors at the second hub, where the replay model puts one of them at the first hub. In Wynberg it puts both connectors at the first hub, where the replay model puts one at each of the first two hubs.

The agent-based search shows strongly falling returns to capacity in Khayelitsha. On the selection seeds, the EV passenger change rises from $-64\%$ at 22~kW to $-18\%$ at the replay design (250~kW) and $-6\%$ at the selected design (580~kW peak on these seeds). The designs with 660 to 860~kW of peak load gain only a further 0.6 to 3.4 percentage points. Under the replay model the same additional capacity has little effect: the selected agent-based design strands 55.1\% of the vehicle-days against 57.0\% for the replay design, because a replayed vehicle cannot divert to use the new connectors.

The selected capacity depends on the tolerance of the selection rule, but the direction of the difference does not (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}). From 90\% to 99\%, the Khayelitsha agent-based design grows from 374 to 1,000~kW of installed power and the replay design from 200 to 350~kW; at every tolerance the agent-based design has more installed power than the replay design in Khayelitsha, the same or less in Bellville, and the same in Wynberg. The upper bound of the design space does not constrain the selection: three Khayelitsha designs above it do not improve the EV passenger change (\ref{app:bound_check}).

Each design evaluated in both models (Table~\ref{tab:two_by_two}) shows that the two models disagree on which design is better in Bellville and Wynberg. There, the agent-based design strands more vehicle-days in the replay model (32.9\% against 22.6\% in Bellville, and 67.3\% against 49.0\% in Wynberg), while the replay design carries fewer EV passengers in the agent-based model. In Khayelitsha, the agent-based design is the better design in both models; the models disagree on whether its extra 400~kW is worth installing.

\subsection{Cost of the replay design and the passengers that the EVs lose (RQ3)}
\label{sec:results_rq3}
% CHANGE: The selection-seed differences are within one point of the fresh-seed values in either direction (R2 self-review #18: in Wynberg they are smaller, so "favour x_A" was not right). "ICE vehicles" -> "ICE competitors" (#14); "of the comparison" -> "of the base case" (#19).

The replay-selected design \(\xR\) is evaluated in \(\MA\) on ten seeds that the search and the selection did not use (Table~\ref{tab:two_by_two}). Its EV passenger change is 11.9, 3.5 and 10.3 percentage points lower than that of \(\xA\) in Khayelitsha, Bellville and Wynberg (standard errors 0.4, 1.0 and 0.7). On the selection seeds the differences were 12.3, 3.5 and 9.7 points, within one point of the fresh-seed values in either direction, so selecting on these seeds did not inflate the lead of \(\xA\). In Khayelitsha the difference comes with 400~kW of additional capacity. In Bellville and Wynberg the two designs have the same installed power and peak site load, so the whole difference of 3.5 and 10.3 points comes from the location of the connectors.

The passengers that the EVs lose do not show up as an increase in the expired requests: the expired-request share changes by at most 0.04 percentage points, and the mean passenger wait by less than 10~s. The paired runs measure where the passengers go. In Khayelitsha the ICE competitors take 97\% (SE 4\%) of the passengers that the EV agents lose under \(\xR\) and 85\% (SE 11\%) under \(\xA\); in Wynberg they take 94\% (SE 10\%) and 98\% (SE 17\%) (Table~\ref{tab:ice_capture}). Where the share is determined, the ICE competitors absorb almost all of the passengers that the EVs do not carry while they charge.

Even under \(\xA\), the EV agents carry 6.1\%, 3.4\% and 16.1\% fewer passengers than in the ICE counterfactual in Khayelitsha, Bellville and Wynberg. When the EV agents' share of the passengers is taken per unit of their share of the time away from home, these losses change by at most 0.7 percentage points, so they do not come from less time on the road. \subsection{When does driver agency matter?}

The models select different designs in all three communities, but the size and the form of the difference depend on three conditions.

First, the EVs must depend on charging at the hubs during the day. One night of home charging does not cover the daily energy need of 94\%--97\% of the replayed vehicle-days, so the time that an EV spends at a charger is potentially taken from passenger service when evaluated under \MA{}. The difference grows with the energy need. With 15\% higher consumption, the cost of \xR{} (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}) rises from 11.9, 3.5 and 10.3 percentage points in the base case to 15.9, 3.9 and 15.1 points in Khayelitsha, Bellville and Wynberg; with 15\% lower consumption, the Bellville difference disappears ($+0.1$ points, SE 1.2; Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}).

Second, how busy the connectors are decides the form of the difference. Waiting in the queue for a free connector is the main way in which charging costs passengers in \MA, and the more \xA{} shortens this wait relative to \xR, the more passengers the EVs keep (Section~\ref{sec:results_rq3}). In Khayelitsha, the EVs draw so much energy from the connectors of \xR{} that every connector would have to charge at full power for 15.2~h a day to supply it. The connectors are therefore occupied for most of the day, only additional connectors can shorten the queue, and \MA{} adds 400~kW. In Bellville and Wynberg, the connectors have spare time, and \MA{} instead puts all of them at one hub at the same installed power. The EVs then share one queue for several connectors instead of a separate queue at each hub. Such a shared queue moves faster than separate queues with the same total number of connectors, which is known as the pooling effect in queueing theory~\cite{smith1981_resource_sharing, mandelbaum1998_pooling_queueing_networks}. The time that the EVs spend in the queue falls by 75\% in Bellville and by 58\% in Wynberg. The replay model rewards the opposite. A replayed vehicle can charge only at a hub where it already stops, so \MR{} spreads the connectors over the hubs at which the observed vehicles stop, and it rates the single-hub design as worse because the vehicles that do not stop at that hub cannot use its connectors (Table~\ref{tab:two_by_two}).

Third, the difference is smallest where the observed vehicles already stop at the hubs. Bellville has the fewest replayed vehicle-days without a stop of 10~min or more within 100~m of a candidate hub (8.1\%, against 28.5\% in Khayelitsha and 33.3\% in Wynberg; Table~\ref{tab:fidelity}), and it has the smallest cost of \xR. The screening does not anticipate this. The binding of hub charging ($B = V_2 - V_1$; Table~\ref{tab:screening}) is largest in Bellville (49.7~pp), but it is large because its vehicles already stop at the hubs, which is the case in which replay is most adequate.

The number of ICE competitors, by contrast, has little effect: halving the scale factor $k$, which sets the size of the ICE population and of the passenger demand, or increasing it by half changes the cost of \xR{} by at most 2.1 percentage points (Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). Competition does not set how many passengers the EVs lose; it sets where those passengers go as further discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion_implications}.

A stronger replay baseline would let a vehicle divert to the nearest charger when its battery is low. Such a rule would remove part of the stranding of \MR{} at a fraction of the computational cost. Without a model of the queue at the charger, however, it would not show the passengers that EVs lose while they wait, and by sending each vehicle to its nearest hub it would not find the single-hub designs of Bellville and Wynberg. A queue-aware diversion rule would close part of the gap, but it would then need the same calibration of when and where drivers charge as the agent-based model.

\subsection{Two models, two verdicts}
% CHANGE: New subsection: the cross-evaluation, the change of the failure mode, and the two models as bounds on driver competence.
% CHANGE: "In M_A, no EV strands" limited to the base case (R2 self-review #19); "ICE taxis" -> "ICE competitors" (#14).

The cross-evaluation shows that the choice of model is not neutral. In Bellville and Wynberg, each model's design is worse in the other model: the agent-based design strands more vehicle-days in \MR, and the replay design carries fewer EV passengers in \MA{} (Table~\ref{tab:two_by_two}). Had both models scored the designs on one shared objective, this disagreement would not have been visible. It appears because each model selects its design using the outcomes that it can represent (stranding in \MR, EV passengers in \MA), and the design is then evaluated in the other model. The model therefore determines which outcomes the planner can see~\cite{sello2026_planning_paradigms}.

The models also differ in how electrification fails. In \MR, an EV fails by running out of energy: under \xR, it strands on 57.0\%, 22.6\% and 49.0\% of the vehicle-days in Khayelitsha, Bellville and Wynberg. In the base case of \MA, no EV strands. Instead, the EVs carry fewer passengers, because their drivers spend time charging and the ICE competitors carry the passengers they miss. The replay model therefore overstates the energy infeasibility of electrification and cannot show its cost in passenger service.

Neither model predicts the outcome of a pilot on its own. The agent-based drivers do not strand because the charging rules give them a reasonable degree of foresight: they check the energy of a route and of the drive to a station before they start it (\ref{app:abm_charging}). \MR{} assumes that drivers do not adapt, and \MA{} that they adapt competently from the first day. Drivers in an early pilot will probably lie between the two, so the models are better read as bounds than as alternatives.

\subsection{Implications for electrification pilots}
\label{sec:discussion_implications}
% CHANGE: Rewritten with the cost model (F114), the location result, the measured ICE capture and the staged use of the two models.
% CHANGE: "ICE taxis take" -> "the ICE competitors take" (R2 self-review #14); "to make a pilot viable" -> "for a pilot to succeed", since "viable" is kept for the sense of Pretorius et al. (#2).

Four implications follow for the planning of pilots. First, where the connectors go matters as much as how many are installed. In Bellville and Wynberg, putting all connectors at one hub keeps 3.5 and 10.3 percentage points more EV passengers than spreading the same installed power over two hubs, and it costs about R40,000 a year less, because one larger installation is cheaper per connector than two smaller ones (Table~\ref{tab:cost_model}).

Second, additional capacity is a close economic decision. In Khayelitsha, the extra 400~kW of the agent-based design pays for itself if each additional EV passenger pays a fare of at least R13.7, just below the fares of R14 to R20 sampled on comparable routes in 2022~\cite{coct2023_citp}. The City of Cape Town's demand and network-capacity charges make up 61\% of the extra cost, so the electricity tariff and any limit on the peak site load affect the decision as much as the cost of the connectors. The selected capacity also depends on how close to the service of the best design the selection rule requires a design to be: from 90\% to 99\% of it, the Khayelitsha design grows from 374 to 1,000~kW (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}). Above about 600~kW of peak site load, each further connector gains little. The robust result is the direction of the change, not its size in kilowatts.

Third, a pilot should monitor the passengers and revenue of the EV operators, not only the service of the system. In Khayelitsha and Wynberg, the ICE competitors take 85\% to 98\% of the passengers that the EVs lose, and the share of passenger requests that expire unserved changes by at most 0.04 percentage points. Measures of the service of the whole system would therefore show no effect, while the EVs still carry 6.1\% fewer passengers in Khayelitsha and 16.1\% fewer in Wynberg even under \xA. This loss falls on the early adopters and risks detering adoption. Careful consideration of the fare and of the revenue of the EV operators is therefore needed for a pilot to succeed.

Fourth, the two models suit different stages of planning. Replay evaluates every design of a community in at most about a minute and suits the screening of communities and the generation of candidate designs. Its own outputs show when the agent-based model is needed: when many vehicle-days strand even with unlimited connectors, when many days have no stop near a candidate hub, and when the connectors would have to charge at full power for most of the day. At pilot scale, the run time of the agent-based analysis (2 to 14~h for a community) is not the main cost; the calibration of behaviour and demand is.

These findings support evaluating candidate designs with both energy-feasibility and passenger-service measures. They do not establish which sites are technically or commercially feasible. The candidate hubs, the design space and the charging assumptions remain a model of a possible pilot.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion_limitations}
% CHANGE: Grouped, with the direction of each effect where it is known. Added: the assumptions of the agent-based model and the Bellville scale factor above the licence bound (Appendix A refers to this section).

\emph{Movement representation.} Calibration reproduces the median daily distance and moving time, and three remaining differences have a known direction. The simulated vehicles spend 1.5 to 2.5~h longer at home overnight than the observed vehicles, which gives the EVs 5 to 8~kWh more home charging a day (6\% to 15\% of the median daily energy need), so \MA{} probably understates the need for hub charging and the passenger losses. The simulated vehicles vary less from day to day, so the extreme days that lengthen the queues are under-represented. The inferred network misses 18.6\% to 41.0\% of the observed driving, most in Wynberg, whose drive-to-charger time and remaining loss are therefore the least certain. The decomposition of Table~\ref{tab:decomposition} shows that the simplified movement does not explain the difference between the selected designs; in Khayelitsha it even works against it.

\emph{Behavioural assumptions.} Drivers queue for a connector in order of arrival and do not know the queue lengths at other hubs, which probably overstates the queue time compared with real drivers who could share this information. Drivers pay no price for charging, and every vehicle with a home charges there at 3.7~kW. The passenger demand is not observed; it is scaled to the full taxi population by $k$, and the Bellville value exceeds the upper bound from the operating licences in the provincial route register. The sensitivity cases from $0.5k$ to $1.5k$ change the cost of \xR{} by at most 2.1 points, so the comparison is robust to this value, although the absolute passenger numbers are not.

\emph{Comparison and cost.} The models measure service differently (stranded vehicle-days in \MR, EV passengers in \MA), so the behaviour-and-selection component of the decomposition contains this change of measure as well as driver adaptation and is not a causal estimate of behaviour alone. The cost model uses US connector costs, which are probably high for South Africa, and fares that are not published for the studied routes, so its result is a break-even fare, not a business case.

\emph{Generalisation.} The screening selects communities partly on their replay outcomes, so the selected communities are those in which replay performs best; this should narrow rather than widen the difference. Three communities in one city and two December weeks cannot establish generalisability across South African minibus-taxi operations or across seasons. As \citet{collett2021_ssa_paratransit_decarbonisation_data} argue, generalisable planning models for paratransit electrification require more systematic data collection.

\subsection{Future work}
% CHANGE: Each item follows from a finding of the study.
% NOTE TO THINUS > I hope this does not undermine the inclusion of the Sebenza dataset, negating its large sample size by using Cape Town. I am trying to make it clear that the key limitation is more temporal.
Four extensions follow from the results. A queue-aware replay with diversion to a charger would show how much of the agent-based result a cheaper model can recover. Reevaluation with candidate sites chosen from where vehicles dwell and operate, rather than the main hubs only, would address the drive-to-charger cost. Constraints and tariffs of the distribution network, together with managed charging, would address the demand charges that dominate the cost of additional capacity. Finally, observations of when and where drivers charge in the first real-world pilots would calibrate the charging behaviour and show where real drivers lie between the two models. A replication with more communities, a larger fleet and other seasons would test the generalisability of the findings.

%   Conclusion
% --------------------------------------------------

\section{Conclusion}
Representing driver adaptation changed charging design selection in all three communities, but the form of the change depended on local operating patterns. In Khayelitsha, the agent-based model selected substantially more installed power; in Bellville and Wynberg, it selected a different connector allocation at the same installed power. Across all three communities, the replay-selected designs carried 3.5 to 11.9 percentage points fewer EV passengers when evaluated in the agent-based model on seeds that the selection did not use, and most of these passengers went to internal-combustion competitors. The additional Khayelitsha capacity pays for itself at a fare of about R14 a trip, and in Bellville and Wynberg the agent-based design is also the cheaper design. These results suggest that replay-based analysis remains useful for evaluating electrification feasibility under observed mobility, but that it may not identify the charging design that best supports passenger service when drivers can adapt. Agent-based simulation can represent these additional operational effects, provided its demand assumptions, behavioural calibration and movement representation are assessed explicitly.
